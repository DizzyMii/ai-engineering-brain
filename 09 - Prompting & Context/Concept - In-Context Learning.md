> **One-paragraph hook:** Show a large model three examples of a task it was never explicitly trained on and it will often get a fourth right, with no gradient step, no optimizer, and not a single weight changed. That's in-context learning (ICL): adaptation that happens inside a forward pass, driven only by what's in the context window. It's one of the most consequential surprises to come out of [[Concept - Scaling Laws]], because one frozen model can be repurposed at inference time just by choosing what to show it. It's the most mechanism-backed corner of [[Concept - Prompt Engineering]].

## The mechanism

Brown et al. (2020), the GPT-3 paper, put ICL on the map. As models scaled, few-shot performance ($k$ input-output exemplars in the prompt, no weight updates) started to rival or approach fine-tuned performance on many tasks. The effect sharpens steeply with model size, which makes it a standard example of an emergent capability (see [[Concept - The Emergent Abilities Debate]]) as opposed to one that scales smoothly.

There are two mechanistic accounts of *why*, and you want both for intuition.

**Mechanism 1: induction heads.** Olsson et al. (2022) found a specific [[Concept - Attention Mechanism]] circuit, the [[Concept - Induction Heads]], that does prefix-match-and-copy. Given `[A][B] ... [A]`, it attends back to the earlier `[A]`, finds what came after it (`[B]`), and copies that forward as the prediction. That's almost literally what few-shot completion needs: the model has seen `input_1 -> output_1, input_2 -> output_2, ..., input_k ->` and has to continue the pattern. Induction heads also form in a visible **phase change** during pretraining, a sudden loss drop together with a jump in in-context learning ability. It's one of the few interpretability findings with a clean causal line from a specific circuit to a specific capability.

**Mechanism 2: implicit optimization / Bayesian inference.** Xie et al. (2022) treat ICL as implicit Bayesian inference. Pretraining exposes the model to a huge mixture of latent "tasks" (documents generated by different underlying processes). At inference time the exemplars act as evidence for which latent task the model is in, and it generates accordingly. Nothing new is learned; an existing capability gets *selected*. A more mechanistic variant, from von Oswald et al. (2023) and Akyurek et al. (2022), shows transformers can run gradient-descent-like weight updates *inside their forward pass* on simple linear tasks. In those restricted settings, in-context learning can literally be an implicit training loop in the activations, which goes beyond retrieval and copying.

## In practice

The result practitioners most need is the surprise from Min et al. (2022): **randomizing the labels** in classification demonstrations barely hurts accuracy. Show a model five sentiment examples with "positive" and "negative" scrambled, and performance drops only modestly. So much of what ICL exemplars provide is the **label space, output format and input distribution**, and much less an input-to-label mapping the model learns fresh. Exemplars mostly teach the model *what shape of answer to give*, and correctness leans heavily on what the model already knew from pretraining. The practical consequence: getting your exemplars' *format* right (see [[Concept - Prompt Formatting and Sensitivity]]) often matters more than getting their *content* perfectly correct.

Shot count saturates. Gains typically plateau somewhere between 1 and 8 shots for most tasks, and every extra shot costs tokens, latency and context budget on every call ([[Concept - Few-Shot Example Selection and Ordering]] covers choosing and ordering the shots you keep). That isn't universal. "Many-shot" ICL (Google, 2024) found that going to hundreds or thousands of shots, which long context windows now make practical, brings back meaningful gains on some tasks where few-shot had plateaued, especially tasks with diverse examples.

## Failure modes

- **Majority-label bias.** The model over-predicts whichever label showed up most among the exemplars, whatever the input. It follows directly from the label-space finding above.
- **Recency bias.** The *last* exemplar's label has outsized influence on the prediction, so exemplar order isn't neutral (see [[Concept - Few-Shot Example Selection and Ordering]]).
- **Format leakage.** If exemplars share an incidental quirk unrelated to the task (say all the positive ones happen to be shorter), the model can take that as the rule.
- **Extreme formatting sensitivity.** Trivial changes like a different delimiter, comma vs. newline, or casing can swing accuracy dramatically for reasons unrelated to task difficulty. [[Concept - Prompt Formatting and Sensitivity]] goes into it; it's one of the more alarming reproducibility findings in the field.

## The non-obvious

Min et al.'s random-label result should change how you debug a failing few-shot prompt. The instinct is to agonize over whether your exemplars are the *most representative* or *most correct*. The evidence says check format consistency and label-space clarity first. With clean formatting and consistent structure, a model often does nearly as well on mediocre exemplars as on perfect ones, and perfect exemplars in an inconsistent or ambiguous format can fail badly. Spend engineering effort accordingly: for most tasks formatting discipline beats exemplar curation. The exception is tasks like [[Concept - Chain-of-Thought and Why It Works|chain-of-thought]] demonstrations, where the exemplar's *content* encodes the solution method and not only the label.
