> **One-paragraph hook:** Show a large model three examples of a task it has never been explicitly trained on, and it will often complete a fourth correctly — with no gradient step, no optimizer, no change to a single weight. In-context learning (ICL) is this capability: adaptation that happens entirely inside a forward pass, purely as a function of what's in the context window. It's one of the most consequential surprises to fall out of [[Concept - Scaling Laws]], because it means a single frozen model can be repurposed at inference time simply by choosing what to show it — the sharpest end of the mechanism-backed side of [[Concept - Prompt Engineering]].

## The mechanism

Brown et al. (2020), introducing GPT-3, is the paper that put ICL on the map: as models scaled, few-shot performance — showing $k$ input-output exemplars in the prompt with no weight updates — began to rival or approach fine-tuned performance on many tasks, and the effect sharpens sharply with model size, making it a hallmark example of an emergent capability (see [[Concept - The Emergent Abilities Debate]]) rather than a smoothly scaling one.

There are two complementary mechanistic accounts of *why* this happens, and both matter for building intuition:

**Mechanism 1 — induction heads.** Olsson et al. (2022) identified a specific [[Concept - Attention Mechanism]] circuit, the [[Concept - Induction Heads]], that does prefix-match-and-copy: given a pattern `[A][B] ... [A]`, the circuit attends back to the earlier occurrence of `[A]`, finds what followed it (`[B]`), and copies that forward as the prediction. This is mechanistically almost exactly what few-shot pattern completion requires — the model has seen `input_1 -> output_1, input_2 -> output_2, ..., input_k ->` and needs to continue the pattern. Strikingly, induction-head formation is a visible **phase change** during pretraining — a sudden drop in loss and simultaneous jump in in-context learning ability, not a gradual acquisition — making this one of the few interpretability findings with a clean causal story linking a specific circuit to a specific capability.

**Mechanism 2 — implicit optimization/Bayesian inference.** Xie et al. (2022) frames ICL as implicit Bayesian inference: pretraining exposes the model to a huge mixture of latent "tasks" (documents implicitly generated by different underlying processes), and at inference time, exemplars in the prompt act as evidence that lets the model infer which latent task it's currently in, then generate accordingly — no new capability is learned, an existing one is *selected*. A more mechanistic variant, von Oswald et al. (2023) and Akyurek et al. (2022), shows that transformers can implement gradient-descent-like weight updates *within their forward pass* on simple linear tasks — meaning "in-context learning" can, in restricted settings, be quite literally an implicit training loop running inside the activations, not just retrieval-and-copy.

## In practice

The single most important empirical result for practitioners is the "surprise" from Min et al. (2022): **randomizing the labels** in classification demonstrations barely hurts accuracy. If you show a model five sentiment examples but scramble which ones say "positive" versus "negative," performance drops only modestly. What this reveals is that a large share of what ICL exemplars provide is the **label space, output format, and input distribution** — not a genuine input-to-label mapping the model is inferring fresh. In other words, exemplars mostly teach the model *what shape of answer to produce*, and correctness leans heavily on capability the model already had from pretraining. This has a direct consequence: getting the *format* of your exemplars exactly right (see [[Concept - Prompt Formatting and Sensitivity]]) often matters more than getting the *content* perfectly correct.

Shot count follows a saturating curve: gains from adding more exemplars typically plateau by somewhere between 1 and 8 shots for most tasks, and every additional shot is a recurring cost in tokens, latency, and consumed context budget (see [[Concept - Few-Shot Example Selection and Ordering]] for how to choose and order the shots you do use). This isn't universal, though — "many-shot" ICL (Google, 2024) showed that pushing into the hundreds-to-thousands-of-shots regime, made newly practical by long-context windows, revives meaningful gains on some tasks where few-shot had already plateaued, particularly tasks with high example diversity.

## Failure modes

- **Majority-label bias.** The model over-predicts whichever label appeared most often among the exemplars, independent of the actual input — a direct consequence of the label-space-not-mapping finding above.
- **Recency bias.** The label of the *last* exemplar shown disproportionately influences the prediction, meaning exemplar order is not a neutral choice (see [[Concept - Few-Shot Example Selection and Ordering]]).
- **Format leakage.** If your exemplars share an incidental formatting quirk unrelated to the task (all positive examples happen to be shorter, say), the model can latch onto that as the "rule" instead of the intended signal.
- **Extreme formatting sensitivity.** Trivial changes — a different delimiter, comma versus newline, casing — can swing accuracy dramatically for reasons that have nothing to do with task difficulty; this is explored in depth in [[Concept - Prompt Formatting and Sensitivity]] and is one of the more alarming reproducibility findings in the field.

## The non-obvious

The Min et al. random-label result should change how you debug a failing few-shot prompt. The instinct is to agonize over whether your exemplars are the *most representative* or *most correct* examples of the task. The evidence says: check the format consistency and label-space clarity first — a model given clean formatting and consistent structure will often perform nearly as well with mediocre exemplars as with perfect ones, while a model given perfect exemplars in an inconsistent or ambiguous format can fail badly. Budget your engineering effort accordingly: formatting discipline beats exemplar curation for most tasks, though not for tasks (like [[Concept - Chain-of-Thought and Why It Works|chain-of-thought]] reasoning demonstrations) where the *content* of the exemplar genuinely encodes the solution method rather than just the label.
