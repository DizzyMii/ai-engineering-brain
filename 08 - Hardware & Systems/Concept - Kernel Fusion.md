# Concept - Kernel Fusion

> **One-paragraph hook:** Every unfused GPU kernel pays a fixed HBM tax regardless of how little arithmetic it does: read its full input tensor from HBM, write its full output tensor back. Chain five elementwise operations — bias add, then GELU, then dropout, then residual add, then a norm — and a framework that dispatches one kernel per op pays that tax five times over, even though the actual computation per element is trivial. Kernel fusion collapses that chain into a single kernel that reads its input once, keeps every intermediate value in registers or [[Concept - GPU Memory Hierarchy|shared memory]], and writes only the final output — turning $N$ read/write passes into essentially one. Because most non-GEMM operations in a transformer are bound by [[Concept - The Memory Wall|HBM bandwidth]], not compute, this is usually the single highest-leverage optimization available before touching an algorithm at all.

## The mechanism

Every kernel launch on a GPU has a fixed structure: read tensor(s) from HBM into on-chip memory, compute, write result tensor(s) back to HBM. For an elementwise or reduction-style operation — anything where the FLOP count per element is small — the read and write dominate the kernel's runtime; the [[Concept - The Roofline Model|roofline model]] formalizes this as low arithmetic intensity. If a computation is expressed as $N$ separate kernel launches, each one performs its own full read-compute-write cycle against HBM: input tensor of size $s$ chained through $N$ ops costs roughly $2N \cdot s$ bytes of HBM traffic (one read, one write, per op) even though the "real" data that needs to move is just the original input and the final output — $2s$ bytes. Fusing those $N$ ops into a single kernel keeps every intermediate value resident in registers or shared memory between operations, cutting the $2N \cdot s$ traffic down toward the theoretical minimum $2s$: a roughly $N\times$ reduction in memory traffic for a chain of $N$ memory-bound ops, and the *actual proof* that a fusion helped is a measured drop in HBM bytes moved, not a FLOP count (FLOPs are typically unchanged or even slightly higher after fusion).

Fusion comes in two shapes. **Vertical fusion** chains a producer directly into a consumer — the output of op A becomes the input of op B without ever leaving the chip, e.g., bias-add feeding directly into an activation function. **Horizontal fusion** combines independent operations that happen to share a kernel launch (same grid, same data locality) even though neither depends on the other's output, saving on launch overhead rather than HBM traffic. Reduction-based fusions — [[Concept - RMSNorm and LayerNorm|LayerNorm/RMSNorm]], softmax — are a special case: they require a full pass over a row to compute a statistic (mean, variance, max, sum) before the per-element output can be computed, so a fused reduction kernel must implement the reduction itself (via shared-memory accumulation or warp-shuffle instructions) rather than simply chaining independent elementwise ops.

## In practice

The canonical fusion targets in a transformer are exactly the operations the [[Concept - The Roofline Model]] flags as memory-bound: bias + activation + dropout in the MLP block, the normalize-and-scale sequence in RMSNorm/LayerNorm, the full softmax reduction, and — the largest and most consequential fusion in the whole stack — the entire multi-stage attention computation, which [[Deep Dive - FlashAttention]] fuses into one kernel using the same "never materialize the intermediate in HBM" logic applied here at the scale of an entire operator rather than a short elementwise chain. Fusion also reaches into the optimizer: a **fused AdamW step** applies the update rule to every parameter in one kernel launch instead of one launch per tensor per sub-operation (compute first moment, compute second moment, bias-correct, update weights), which matters enormously at the scale of a model with thousands of parameter tensors where launch overhead alone becomes material.

Tooling has mostly automated this. **`torch.compile`/TorchInductor** traces a model's forward (and backward) graph and auto-generates fused [[Concept - Triton]] kernels for eligible op chains; **nvFuser** does similar graph-level fusion inside the PyTorch/JIT stack; XLA performs whole-program fusion as a first-class compiler pass for TPU workloads. Hand-written fusion in Triton or raw CUDA remains common for operators the auto-fusers don't reach — attention being the primary example, which is why FlashAttention is hand-engineered rather than compiler-generated. Measured wins are concrete and unglamorous: fusing a norm-plus-residual-add sequence typically cuts that block's wall-clock time by roughly 2-3x, purely from HBM traffic reduction — no new algorithm, no new math, just fewer trips to memory.

## Failure modes

- **Fusing across a large GEMM for no benefit**: a large matmul is already compute-bound (high arithmetic intensity from [[Concept - Matmul Tiling on GPUs|GEMM tiling]]), so fusing an elementwise op *into* the GEMM's epilogue helps (that's free — the output tile is already on-chip), but trying to fuse two independent large GEMMs together buys nothing, since neither was memory-bound to begin with. Detection: profiling shows no reduction in achieved HBM throughput after the "optimization," because there was no HBM-bound bottleneck to remove.
- **Register pressure and occupancy collapse**: fusing more operations into one kernel means the kernel needs more live values in registers simultaneously (every intermediate in the chain), which can push register usage per thread high enough to reduce [[Concept - Occupancy and Latency Hiding|occupancy]] or trigger spilling to local memory — turning a fusion meant to reduce HBM traffic into a kernel that reintroduces HBM traffic through spills. Detection: `ptxas -v` reporting spill loads/stores, or a fused kernel that's *slower* than the sum of its unfused parts.
- **The recompute-vs-materialize tradeoff going the wrong way**: aggressive fusion sometimes implies recomputing an intermediate value multiple times inside the kernel rather than storing it once, which is correct when the intermediate is cheap to recompute and expensive to store (this is exactly FlashAttention's backward-pass bet) but wrong when the intermediate is itself expensive — a fusion strategy copied from one operator to a structurally different one without re-checking this tradeoff quietly regresses performance.
- **Correctness drift from precision mismatches**: a hand-fused kernel that accumulates a reduction (sum, mean, variance) in a lower precision than the reference unfused implementation used can produce numerically different results — not wrong per se, but different enough to break a regression test comparing fused vs. unfused outputs bit-for-bit, a common false alarm that's actually a legitimate precision choice.

## The non-obvious

The instinct when a model is slow is to look for a faster algorithm; for the large majority of the non-GEMM operations in a transformer, the actual bottleneck was never the algorithm, it was the number of times data crossed the HBM boundary. This reframes performance debugging: before assuming a kernel needs smarter math, check whether it needs *fewer trips to memory* instead — and the diagnostic for that is not a stopwatch on the op, it's [[Concept - The Roofline Model|roofline]] classification (is this kernel's achieved throughput near the memory ceiling or the compute ceiling?). The second non-obvious point is that fusion has a ceiling of its own: once a chain of ops is fully fused down to $2s$ bytes of HBM traffic, you are at the memory-bound floor for that chain, and no further fusion helps — at that point the only way to go faster is to change what data needs to move at all, which is a step up in ambition from fusion to a genuinely different algorithm (again, FlashAttention's move: it doesn't just fuse the existing attention formula, it restructures the computation with online softmax so the $O(N^2)$ intermediate never needs to exist in the first place).
