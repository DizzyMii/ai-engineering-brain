# Playbook - Measuring AI ROI

> **Goal:** produce an AI ROI figure that would survive a skeptical CFO or auditor, not a number generated by comparing a vibe to a headline. **When to run this:** before an AI pilot's budget is scaled to production, before any ROI number leaves the building publicly, and every quarter thereafter for anything already in production. **Prerequisites:** a documented pre-AI baseline (or the ability to construct one retroactively from historical records), production usage logs ([[Concept - LLM Observability and Tracing]]), and the ability to run an A/B or holdout rather than a straight before/after comparison.

## Steps

1. **Baseline the human/legacy process before you deploy.** Action: measure the pre-AI process's cost, time, and quality/error rate on a representative task sample. Expected observation: a documented number (e.g., "$X per ticket resolved, Y minutes median, Z% error rate") that predates the AI system going live. What deviation means: if this step is skipped and the AI is deployed first, there is no pre-AI number to compare against — any post-hoc ROI claim is unfalsifiable by construction, which is the single most common reason claimed AI "ROI" doesn't survive scrutiny (see [[Concept - The Pilot-to-Production Gap]]).

2. **Compute the fully-loaded cost, not just the inference bill.** Action: sum inference tokens and retries, build/integration engineering time (amortized), prompt/eval maintenance, human oversight and review time, and error-remediation cost. Expected observation: fully-loaded cost typically runs several multiples of raw token spend once oversight and remediation are counted. What deviation means: if your total cost is roughly equal to your token spend, you are almost certainly undercounting the hidden denominator — the oversight and remediation line items that turn a positive pilot negative at scale (see [[Concept - Unit Economics of LLM Products]]).

3. **Attribute the effect with a counterfactual — an A/B or holdout, not a before/after.** Action: randomize a matched set of tasks or users into AI-assisted and non-AI-assisted conditions and compare outcomes on the same time window. Expected observation: an effect size with a confidence interval, not a single point estimate from comparing "before" to "after." What deviation means: before/after comparisons confound seasonality, other simultaneous process changes, and self-selection. This failure mode is not hypothetical: METR's July 2025 randomized trial found experienced developers took **19% longer** to complete real coding tasks when using AI tools, while those same developers *believed*, afterward, that AI had made them **~20% faster** (E3, METR, n=16, 246 tasks) — self-reported productivity and measured productivity pointed in opposite directions. METR's own February 2026 follow-up then found the effect shrank to a statistically insignificant **-4%** (CI: -15% to +9%) once developer refusal to work without AI (30-50% declined non-AI tasks even at $50/hour) worsened the sample's self-selection bias — METR itself now flags the 2025 headline number "out of date." The lesson is not "AI helps" or "AI hurts," it's that **only a controlled counterfactual, run and re-run, catches an effect this easy to get backwards from self-report** (see [[Breakdown - The METR Developer Slowdown RCT]], [[Reference - Developer Productivity Studies]]).

4. **Pick outcome metrics that survive Goodhart's law.** Action: track outcomes that require the result to *hold* — resolved-and-stayed-resolved tickets, merged-and-not-reverted pull requests — never proxy metrics like messages sent or suggestions accepted. Expected observation: the Goodhart-resistant metric and the easy proxy metric diverge over time as usage matures. What deviation means: if the only dashboard is acceptance rate or engagement, the measurement is tracking adoption, not value delivered, and can rise while true outcomes stay flat or worsen (see [[Concept - The Evaluation Gap]]).

5. **Subtract the quality and liability tail.** Action: net out any downstream cost the gross savings created — support deflection that raises churn, AI-authored code or content that raises defect/rework rate, and hallucination-driven liability events. Expected observation: net savings are lower than gross savings, sometimes substantially. What deviation means: a reported ROI that only shows gross savings, with no tail-cost line, has not actually been measured against a liability side — see [[Lore - Hallucination Liability Incidents]] for the incident-level cost this tail can carry.

6. **Sanity-check against the public spread, then trust your own number over either end of it.** Action: compare your figure to the two most-cited public benchmarks — IDC's Microsoft-commissioned "$3.70 returned per $1 spent" (E1, vendor-sponsored survey, Jan 2025) and MIT Project NANDA's finding that ~95% of enterprise gen-AI pilots show no measurable P&L impact (E2, Jul-Aug 2025). Expected observation: your measured number sits somewhere explicable given your own deployment's maturity and integration depth, not exactly matching either headline. What deviation means: a number that suspiciously matches a vendor-sponsored benchmark, or one that simply declares "we're in the 5%" without the steps above to back it, has not actually been measured (full context in [[Reference - AI Impact by Business Function]]).

7. **Recompute quarterly on real production data.** Action: rerun steps 1-6 with updated token prices and updated usage volume, not the pilot-era numbers. Expected observation: token cost falling ([[Concept - Token Price Deflation]]) helps the cost side, but agentic and reasoning workloads consuming far more tokens per task than the pilot's chat-style usage can worsen the volume side faster than deflation helps. What deviation means: an ROI figure computed once at pilot stage and never revisited is stale by definition — a pilot's ROI rarely survives contact with production scale, in either direction, and only recomputation catches which way it moved.

## Verification

The resulting ROI number is defensible only if all four hold: (a) it is measured against a documented pre-deployment baseline, not inferred after the fact; (b) it comes from a counterfactual (A/B or holdout) rather than a before/after comparison; (c) it is net of fully-loaded cost and the quality/liability tail, not gross savings; (d) rerunning the same procedure on a fresh quarter of production data produces a similar number, or a difference you can explain (token price change, usage mix change, workflow change). If any of the four is missing, the number is a claim, not a measurement — tier it E0/E1 accordingly rather than stating it as fact, per the Applied Wing evidence law.

## When it goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Jump to fix |
|---|---|---|
| ROI looks great in the deck but finance can't locate the savings in the actual budget | No pre-AI baseline was ever measured — the "savings" numerator is inferred, not observed | Step 1 |
| ROI collapses once the pilot scales from a small team to the whole org | Fully-loaded cost undercounted oversight/build/maintenance cost that scales with headcount | Step 2 |
| Users report large time savings, but throughput or output metrics don't move | Self-report bias — the same pattern METR measured (perceived speedup, measured slowdown or null effect) | Step 3 |
| Adoption/engagement metrics are up, but no one can point to a business outcome that changed | Tracking proxy metrics (usage, acceptance) instead of Goodhart-resistant outcome metrics | Step 4 |
| Reported net savings quietly evaporate a quarter or two after rollout | Quality/liability tail (rework, churn, incidents) was never subtracted, or cost/usage assumptions went stale | Steps 5 and 7 |
