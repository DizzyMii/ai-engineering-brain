> **One-paragraph hook:** Give a transformer the sequence `... A B ... A` and it will predict `B` next, often with high confidence, even if it has never seen that specific pair before. This is not a lookup table and it is not magic — it is a two-head circuit built entirely from ordinary [[Concept - Attention Mechanism|attention]] composition, and it is the single most thoroughly reverse-engineered mechanism in a transformer. Induction heads matter to this domain for two reasons: they are the field's strongest existence proof that [[Deep Dive - Mechanistic Interpretability|mechanistic interpretability]] can explain a real capability end to end, and the exact same circuit that makes in-context learning useful is what a specific jailbreak exploits to defeat safety training.

## The mechanism

The circuit is two heads working in different layers, composed through the residual stream. Layer $\ell_1$ contains a **previous-token head**: at position $i$, it attends almost entirely to position $i-1$ and writes that token's identity forward into the residual stream at position $i$. Layer $\ell_2$ (strictly later) contains the **induction head** proper, and it reads what the previous-token head just wrote via **K-composition** — its key vector at a candidate position is partly a function of the previous-token head's output, not just the raw embedding at that position. Concretely: at the current position (token `A`, the second occurrence), the induction head's query asks "which earlier position has, as *its* previous token, the token I am currently at" — and because the previous-token head already broadcast "my previous token was `A`" onto the position right after the *first* occurrence of `A`, that is exactly the position the query matches. This is **prefix matching**. Having attended there, the induction head's **OV circuit** copies the *content* — not the identity, the value — sitting at that matched position (which is `B`) into its output, directly promoting `B`'s logit at the current position. K-composition finds *where* to look; the OV circuit determines *what* gets copied once you're looking there.

Olsson et al. (2022, "In-context Learning and Induction Heads," Anthropic) is the paper that nailed this down, and the evidence is unusually clean for interpretability: plotting training loss against tokens seen shows a visible bump — a brief, sharp phase change roughly in the 2.5B-5B token range of training — and that bump coincides almost exactly with the moment induction heads form in the attention patterns *and* with a jump in the model's in-context learning ability, measured as the gap between loss on the 500th token of a context versus the 50th. Three independently measurable things (a training-loss kink, an attention-pattern signature, and an ICL-ability jump) move together at the same point in training, which is much stronger evidence than any one of them alone.

Correlation isn't causation, so the paper also **ablates**: knocking out the identified induction heads produces a sharp, disproportionate drop in ICL performance relative to ablating an equal number of random heads — the standard move (formalized later as [[Concept - Activation Patching|activation patching]]) for turning "this component's activity correlates with the behavior" into "this component is causally responsible for the behavior." The minimal model that reliably forms this circuit is a two-layer, attention-only transformer with no MLPs at all — small enough to fully enumerate every head's QK and OV behavior by hand, which is exactly why induction heads are the canonical first exercise in TransformerLens tutorials and most mechanistic-interpretability onboarding.

Real models generalize the pattern past exact-token repeats: **fuzzy** or **soft** induction heads perform approximate matching (near-synonyms, not just identical tokens) and **translated** copying (matching on one representation, copying a related-but-different one — e.g., pattern-completing across a template rather than a literal string), scaffolding richer forms of pattern continuation and few-shot task inference on top of the same K-composition/OV-copy skeleton.

## In practice

Induction heads are treated as a load-bearing explanation for [[Concept - Chain-of-Thought and Why It Works|in-context learning]] broadly, not a curiosity: they are one of the few places in a real model where "this specific circuit does this specific interpretable computation" survives careful ablation testing rather than dissolving under scrutiny. Head implementations vary with attention layout — [[Concept - Multi-Head Attention Variants (MHA MQA GQA MLA)|GQA and MQA's shared key/value heads]] change how many independent prefix-matching queries a given layer can run in parallel versus how many distinct KV projections are available to match against, a detail that matters when hunting for induction heads in a modern production-scale attention layout rather than the toy attention-only models the original circuit was found in. Practically, hunting for candidate induction heads starts cheaply — look for heads whose attention pattern shows the diagonal-offset signature of prefix matching on repeated-token synthetic sequences — but confirming one is real requires the ablation step; attention-pattern-matching alone produces false positives (below).

## Failure modes

- **Mistaking "attention pattern looks like induction" for "is an induction head."** Several heads in a real model develop attention patterns that superficially resemble prefix matching without the OV circuit actually copying the right content, or the effect is redundant with other heads. Detection: never certify a head from its attention pattern alone — run the ablation and confirm a measurable drop in ICL-style completions, the same caution the field learned the hard way about attention weights not being faithful explanations in general.
- **Attributing all of in-context learning to induction heads.** Ablating the identified induction heads reduces ICL sharply but does not zero it out — other mechanisms (task vectors, later-layer semantic composition) contribute too, and induction heads are best understood as the dominant, best-understood *component* of ICL, not its entire explanation. Detection: report the ablation delta, not an assumed complete removal.
- **Treating the circuit as purely beneficial.** [[Concept - Many-Shot Jailbreaking|Many-shot jailbreaking]] rides this exact mechanism: fill the context with dozens of fabricated turns where the assistant complies with harmful requests, and the same in-context pattern-completion machinery that makes few-shot prompting work generalizes the "comply" pattern to the real request at the end. You cannot patch this away without weakening the induction mechanism that also makes benign in-context learning work — a genuine capability/safety entanglement, not a bug you can isolate and fix.

## The non-obvious

Before induction heads, "in-context learning" was frequently described as an emergent, faintly mysterious property that showed up as models got large — see [[Concept - The Emergent Abilities Debate]] for the broader argument about what "emergence" claims actually mean. Olsson et al. reframe a meaningful chunk of that mystery as a specific, small, hand-auditable algorithm that forms at a specific point in training and can be turned off with a scalpel. That reframing is the paper's real contribution: it demonstrates that at least one headline "emergent" capability is not an opaque scaling phenomenon but a concrete circuit anyone can trace, which is why induction heads are cited as the strongest single argument that circuit-level interpretability is tractable at all — comparable in spirit to how [[Concept - Grokking|grokking's]] clean Fourier algorithm for modular addition showed a network's internals can be fully legible, not just its inputs and outputs.
