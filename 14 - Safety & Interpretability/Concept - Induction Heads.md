> **One-paragraph hook:** Give a transformer the sequence `... A B ... A` and it predicts `B` next, often with high confidence, even if it has never seen that pair before. It isn't a lookup table. It's a two-head circuit built entirely from ordinary [[Concept - Attention Mechanism|attention]] composition, and it's the most thoroughly reverse-engineered mechanism in a transformer. Induction heads matter here for two reasons. They're the field's strongest existence proof that [[Deep Dive - Mechanistic Interpretability|mechanistic interpretability]] can explain a real capability end to end. And the same circuit that makes in-context learning useful is what one jailbreak exploits to get around safety training.

## The mechanism

Two heads in different layers, composed through the residual stream. Layer $\ell_1$ has a **previous-token head**: at position $i$ it attends almost entirely to position $i-1$ and writes that token's identity forward into the residual stream at $i$. Layer $\ell_2$, strictly later, has the **induction head** itself. It reads what the previous-token head wrote through **K-composition**, meaning its key vector at a candidate position depends partly on the previous-token head's output and not only on the raw embedding there.

Walk through it. At the current position (token `A`, second occurrence), the induction head's query asks which earlier position has, as *its* previous token, the token I'm on now. The previous-token head already wrote "my previous token was `A`" onto the position right after the *first* `A`, so that's the position the query matches. This is **prefix matching**. Having attended there, the induction head's **OV circuit** copies the *content* at the matched position (the value, which is `B`, not the position's identity) into its output, directly boosting `B`'s logit. K-composition decides *where* to look; the OV circuit decides *what* gets copied from there.

Olsson et al. (2022, "In-context Learning and Induction Heads," Anthropic) nailed this down, and the evidence is unusually clean for interpretability. Plot training loss against tokens seen and there's a visible bump, a brief, sharp phase change roughly in the 2.5B-5B token range. It lines up almost exactly with induction heads appearing in the attention patterns *and* with a jump in in-context learning ability, measured as the loss gap between the 500th and the 50th token of a context. Three independently measurable things (a training-loss kink, an attention-pattern signature, an ICL-ability jump) move together at the same point, which is far stronger evidence than any one alone.

Correlation isn't causation, so the paper also **ablates**. Knocking out the identified induction heads drops ICL performance sharply and disproportionately, compared with ablating the same number of random heads. That's the standard move, later formalized as [[Concept - Activation Patching|activation patching]], for getting from "this component's activity correlates with the behavior" to "this component causes it." The smallest model that reliably forms the circuit is a two-layer, attention-only transformer with no MLPs. You can enumerate every head's QK and OV behavior by hand, which is why induction heads are the standard first exercise in TransformerLens tutorials and most mechanistic-interpretability onboarding.

Real models extend the pattern past exact repeats. **Fuzzy** or **soft** induction heads do approximate matching (near-synonyms as well as identical tokens), and **translated** copying matches on one representation and copies a related but different one, e.g. completing a pattern across a template instead of a literal string. Richer pattern continuation and few-shot task inference build on the same K-composition/OV-copy skeleton.

## In practice

Induction heads are treated as a core explanation for [[Concept - Chain-of-Thought and Why It Works|in-context learning]] broadly. They're one of the few places in a real model where "this circuit does this interpretable computation" survives careful ablation instead of dissolving under scrutiny. Implementations vary with attention layout. [[Concept - Multi-Head Attention Variants (MHA MQA GQA MLA)|GQA and MQA's shared key/value heads]] change how many independent prefix-matching queries a layer can run in parallel versus how many distinct KV projections there are to match against. That matters when you hunt for induction heads in a modern production-scale attention layout instead of the toy attention-only models where the circuit was first found. The hunt starts cheap: look for heads whose attention shows the diagonal-offset signature of prefix matching on repeated-token synthetic sequences. Confirming one takes the ablation step, because pattern matching alone gives false positives (below).

## Failure modes

- **Treating "attention pattern looks like induction" as "is an induction head."** Several heads in a real model develop patterns that look like prefix matching while the OV circuit doesn't copy the right content, or the effect is redundant with other heads. Detection: never certify a head from its attention pattern alone. Run the ablation and confirm a measurable drop in ICL-style completions. The field learned this caution the hard way about attention weights not being faithful explanations in general.
- **Crediting all of in-context learning to induction heads.** Ablating them cuts ICL sharply but doesn't zero it. Other mechanisms (task vectors, later-layer semantic composition) contribute, and induction heads are best seen as the dominant, best-understood *component* of ICL, not the full explanation. Detection: report the ablation delta; don't assume complete removal.
- **Treating the circuit as purely beneficial.** [[Concept - Many-Shot Jailbreaking|Many-shot jailbreaking]] rides this mechanism. Fill the context with dozens of fabricated turns where the assistant complies with harmful requests, and the pattern-completion machinery that makes few-shot prompting work carries the "comply" pattern to the real request at the end. You can't patch this without weakening the induction mechanism that benign in-context learning also uses. Capability and safety are tangled together here; it isn't a bug you can isolate.

## The non-obvious

Before induction heads, in-context learning was often described as an emergent, faintly mysterious property that appeared as models got large (see [[Concept - The Emergent Abilities Debate]] for the wider argument about what "emergence" claims mean). Olsson et al. turned a good chunk of that mystery into a specific, small, hand-auditable algorithm that forms at a specific point in training and can be switched off with a scalpel. That's the paper's real contribution: at least one headline "emergent" capability is a concrete circuit anyone can trace, not an opaque scaling phenomenon. It's why induction heads get cited as the strongest single argument that circuit-level interpretability is tractable at all, much as [[Concept - Grokking|grokking's]] clean Fourier algorithm for modular addition showed a network's internals, and not only its inputs and outputs, can be fully legible.
