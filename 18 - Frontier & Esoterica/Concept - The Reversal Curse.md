> **One-paragraph hook:** Fine-tune a model on "Uriah Hawthorne is the composer of Abyssal Melodies" and ask it "Who composed Abyssal Melodies?" — chance-level accuracy, not the near-100% you'd expect from a fact it was just trained on. LLMs store facts directionally, keyed on the order they appeared in training, and this isn't a minor training-data quirk you can paraphrase your way out of — it's a structural property of autoregressive training that shows up at GPT-4 scale and has direct consequences for knowledge editing, synthetic data design, and why parametric memory alone is not enough.

## The mechanism
Berglund et al. 2023 ("The Reversal Curse: LLMs trained on 'A is B' fail to learn 'B is A'") fine-tuned models via [[Concept - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)]] on synthetic facts of the form "A is B" — fictional people and their fictional occupations, deliberately novel so there's no chance the reverse fact leaked in from pretraining — then tested the reverse direction, "Who is B?" The model's accuracy and log-probability for the *correct* answer in the reverse direction were statistically no better than for a random name: the model genuinely never formed the reverse association, not "formed it weakly."

The mechanism is a direct consequence of how [[Concept - Backpropagation]] updates weights under next-token prediction. Training on "A is B" only ever computes and backpropagates the gradient for $P(B \mid \text{context ending in A})$ — the loss function never constructs, and gradient descent never sees, the objective $P(A \mid \text{context ending in B})$. If facts are stored in the MLP layers as something like directional key→value lookups (key: "A", value: "B"), the training process that would write the reverse key ("B" → "A") is simply never run. Association is asymmetric by construction, not by an accident of insufficient data — it's baked into what autoregressive pretraining optimizes for. This is distinct from [[Concept - Induction Heads]], the in-context copying circuitry that lets a model use a fact *within* a single context regardless of the direction it's presented in — the curse is specifically about what gets baked into weights via pretraining gradients, not what can be done in-context at inference time.

Notably, this isn't fixed by scale or paraphrase augmentation within the range Berglund et al. studied — bigger models and rephrasing the same fact multiple ways during training don't induce the reverse association, because rephrasing "A is B" a dozen different ways still only ever trains the forward direction.

## In practice
The effect survives at frontier scale on real-world, naturally-occurring facts, not just synthetic fine-tuning setups. GPT-4 answers "Who is Tom Cruise's mother?" correctly around 79% of the time, but "Who is Mary Lee Pfeiffer's son?" — the same fact, reverse direction — only about 33% of the time. The gap is smallest for name pairs where both directions happen to co-occur densely in pretraining data (celebrities discussed both ways constantly) and largest for facts almost always stated in one canonical direction.

## Failure modes
The most consequential downstream failure is in **knowledge editing**: if you patch a model to know a new or corrected fact by writing it in one direction, that edit does not propagate to the reverse query — you have to explicitly edit both directions, doubling the surface area of every factual update and making "the model now knows X" a direction-qualified claim rather than a general one. This is a close cousin of [[Concept - Catastrophic Forgetting]], since updating a model's knowledge is brittle and narrowly scoped in both cases. It is also a concrete argument for why [[Deep Dive - RAG Architectures]] remains necessary even as context windows and parametric capacity grow: retrieval re-presents a fact in whatever direction the query needs at inference time, sidestepping asymmetric parametric storage entirely rather than hoping the model happened to memorize both directions. The directional-storage finding is one of several unresolved threads under hallucination-as-open-problem in [[Reference - Open Problems in LLM Engineering]].

## The non-obvious
The reversal curse is a special case of a broader, more unsettling pattern: models store what they were trained to *predict*, not the logical closure of what they were trained on. Allen-Zhu & Li's "Physics of Language Models" line of work on knowledge extraction makes the same point from a different angle — there is a real gap between whether a fact is *stored* somewhere in the weights (probeable, present in some representation among the overlapping feature directions described in [[Concept - Superposition]]) and whether it is *extractable* by ordinary generation (retrievable via the forward-prediction pathway the model actually uses at inference). The reversal curse says the gap can be total, not partial: for a genuinely one-directional fact, the reverse isn't stored-but-hard-to-extract, it's simply absent as an association the forward-only gradient never wrote. The practical lesson for anyone building [[Concept - Synthetic Training Data]]: state every fact you care about in both directions explicitly, because "the model will infer the symmetric case" is false, not merely weak.
