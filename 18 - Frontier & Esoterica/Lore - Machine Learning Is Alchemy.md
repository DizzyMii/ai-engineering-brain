## What happened
At NeurIPS 2017, Ali Rahimi took the stage to accept the **Test-of-Time award** for his 2007 random-features paper — a prize given a decade later to the work whose influence held up best. Instead of a victory lap, he used the platform to indict the field he'd just been honored by. His line, now one of the most quoted sentences in machine learning: *"Machine learning has become alchemy."* His argument was specific, not just a mood: too much of the field's progress comes from trial-and-error tweaking of architectures, optimizers, and hyperparameters until something works, followed by post-hoc stories about why — without ablations, without controls, without the kind of rigor that would let you tell which part of a change actually mattered. He wasn't asking people to stop building things that work; he was asking for the discipline to know *why* they work, the way chemistry eventually grew out of alchemy's trial-and-error and gave you predictive theory instead of recipes.

Yann LeCun's public response, posted shortly after, pushed back hard — and the pushback was itself substantive, not just defensive. LeCun argued that calling an entire community's methodology "alchemy" was corrosive and, in his words, "dangerous," because it delegitimizes empirical engineering that has a long and honorable precedent: alchemy *did* give rise to chemistry and metallurgy, and throughout the history of engineering, working artifacts have routinely preceded the theory that explains them — the steam engine ran for decades before thermodynamics could tell you why; the telescope predated optical theory adequate to derive its design. In this framing, "we don't have a clean theory yet" is not evidence of malpractice, it's the normal condition of any field pushing the frontier of what can be built versus what can be proven.

Both men were right about something real, and the argument never resolved — it just became the permanent background hum of the field. Nearly a decade later, the tension is sharper, not smaller: labs now ship trillion-parameter systems whose core training dynamics — [[Concept - Grokking]], [[Concept - Double Descent]], the sharp capability jumps debated in the emergent-abilities literature — cannot be predicted from first principles before you run the experiment. You train the model, watch what happens, and write the paper afterward explaining (partially) why.

## The lesson
This is the founding argument for why this domain — frontier and esoterica — exists at all, and why it is allowed to be full of things you cannot derive. The mechanical lesson: when a field's artifacts outrun its theory, the working knowledge that keeps practitioners from repeatedly rediscovering the same failure modes lives in **folklore** — passed-down heuristics, war stories, "we tried X and it broke Y," conference-hallway consensus that never made it into a paper because it's not a clean enough result to publish. Rich Sutton's essay "The Bitter Lesson" (2019) sharpens the same point from a different angle: across the history of AI, general methods that leverage more computation have consistently beaten methods built on hand-engineered human knowledge of the domain, meaning that even when researchers *do* have a theoretical story for why a structural prior should help, brute-force scaling with less structure tends to win anyway — a second data point for why clean theory keeps losing to empirical scaling as the field's actual engine of progress.

The practitioner-facing takeaway is not "abandon rigor" — Rahimi's ablations-and-controls point stands and this vault applies it wherever theory does exist (see how [[Concept - Grokking]] is tied down mechanistically via progress measures, or how [[Concept - Double Descent]] has a precise interpolation-threshold account). The takeaway is that **labeled folklore is legitimate knowledge, not a lesser substitute for theory** — a documented "we don't know why but here's what reliably happens" (see [[Lore - Hyperparameter Folklore]] for a catalog of exactly this) is more valuable to an engineer than a false certainty borrowed from a theory that doesn't actually cover the regime you're operating in. Cataloging the alchemy honestly, with the confidence level attached, is this domain's actual job.

## Evidence status
Verified history. The Test-of-Time talk and LeCun's rebuttal are both public record — the talk was recorded and widely circulated, and LeCun's response was posted openly and is extensively cited in retrospectives on the exchange. The word "alchemy" itself is deliberately provocative rhetoric chosen for a live audience, not a precise technical claim — treat the framing as a rallying point for a real methodological debate, not as a peer-reviewed thesis. Sutton's "Bitter Lesson" is a real, widely-read essay (not folklore) but its predictive force going forward is itself contested — labeled here as a load-bearing argument in the same tradition, not a settled law.
