## What happened
At NeurIPS 2017, Ali Rahimi went on stage to accept the **Test-of-Time award** for his 2007 random-features paper, a prize given a decade later to the work whose influence held up best. He skipped the victory lap and used the platform to indict the field that had just honored him. His line is now one of the most quoted in machine learning: *"Machine learning has become alchemy."* The argument was specific. Too much of the field's progress came from tweaking architectures, optimizers and hyperparameters by trial and error until something worked, then telling post-hoc stories about why, with no ablations, no controls, none of the rigor that would show which part of a change mattered. He wasn't asking people to stop building things that work. He wanted the discipline to know *why* they work, the way chemistry eventually grew out of alchemy's trial and error and replaced recipes with predictive theory.

Yann LeCun's public response, posted shortly after, pushed back hard, and with substance. He called it corrosive and, in his word, "dangerous" to label a whole community's methodology alchemy, because it delegitimizes empirical engineering with a long and honorable precedent. Alchemy *did* give rise to chemistry and metallurgy. Across engineering history, working artifacts have routinely come before the theory that explains them: the steam engine ran for decades before thermodynamics could say why, and the telescope predated optical theory good enough to derive its design. On this view, "we don't have a clean theory yet" isn't evidence of malpractice. It's the normal condition of any field building things faster than it can prove things about them.

Both were right about something, and the argument never resolved. It became the permanent background hum of the field. Nearly a decade on, the tension is sharper. Labs ship trillion-parameter systems whose core training dynamics ([[Concept - Grokking]], [[Concept - Double Descent]], the sharp capability jumps argued over in the emergent-abilities literature) can't be predicted from first principles before running the experiment. You train the model, watch what happens, and afterward write the paper explaining (partly) why.

## The lesson
This argument is why this domain, frontier and esoterica, exists at all, and why it's allowed to be full of things you can't derive. When a field's artifacts outrun its theory, the knowledge that stops practitioners rediscovering the same failures lives in **folklore**: handed-down heuristics, war stories, "we tried X and it broke Y," hallway consensus that never reached a paper because the result wasn't clean enough to publish. Rich Sutton's essay "The Bitter Lesson" (2019) makes a related point. Across AI's history, general methods that use more computation have consistently beaten methods built on hand-engineered domain knowledge. Even when researchers *do* have a theoretical story for why a structural prior should help, brute-force scaling with less structure tends to win anyway, which is a second reason clean theory keeps losing to empirical scaling as the field's engine of progress.

For practitioners the takeaway isn't "abandon rigor." Rahimi's point about ablations and controls stands, and this vault applies it wherever theory exists: [[Concept - Grokking]] is pinned down mechanistically through progress measures, and [[Concept - Double Descent]] has a precise interpolation-threshold account. The takeaway is that **labeled folklore is legitimate knowledge, not a second-rate substitute for theory**. A documented "we don't know why, but here's what reliably happens" ([[Lore - Hyperparameter Folklore]] is a catalog of exactly that) is worth more to an engineer than false certainty borrowed from a theory that doesn't cover the regime you're in. Cataloging the alchemy honestly, with confidence levels attached, is what this domain is for.

## Evidence status
Verified history. The Test-of-Time talk and LeCun's rebuttal are public record: the talk was recorded and widely circulated, and LeCun posted his response openly; it's cited heavily in retrospectives on the exchange. "Alchemy" was deliberately provocative rhetoric for a live audience, not a precise technical claim. Treat it as a rallying point for a real methodological debate, not a peer-reviewed thesis. Sutton's "Bitter Lesson" is a real, widely read essay (not folklore), but its predictive force going forward is itself contested. It's labeled here as a central argument in the same tradition, not a settled law.
