> **One-paragraph hook:** Ask a frontier model whether 9.11 or 9.9 is larger and a shocking fraction say 9.11. This is not a reasoning failure in the way it looks — it is a **tokenization** failure leaking upward into what appears to be arithmetic. How a tokenizer chops numbers into tokens determines whether the model can even *see* the digit structure it needs to add, compare, or count, and for arithmetic-heavy workloads the tokenizer's digit handling can matter more than the model's parameter count. This note is the folklore of number tokenization: why byte-pair encoding mutilates numbers, what the labs did about it, and the failure signatures that look like reasoning bugs but are really artifacts of a merge table.

## The mechanism
The root problem is that byte-level [[Concept - Byte-Pair Encoding]] builds its vocabulary by merging the most *frequent* adjacent substrings in the training corpus — and it has no idea that digits are a positional, compositional system. Frequency, not arithmetic structure, decides the merges. So `2017` (a common year) becomes a single token, while `2018` might tokenize as `201` + `8`, and `31415` might be `31` + `415` or `314` + `15` depending entirely on which substrings the corpus happened to make frequent. The consequences are brutal for numeracy:
- **Identical digits get inconsistent, non-compositional representations.** The digit `7` in `7` , in `2017`, and in `700000` may live inside three different token ids, so the model cannot reuse a single "what a 7 in the units place does" circuit.
- **Carry/borrow structure is invisible.** Addition requires aligning digits by place value and propagating carries. If `456` and `789` are each single opaque tokens, there is no per-digit column for a carry to propagate along; the model must memorize sums of token-pairs instead of computing them.

This is precisely the kind of representation problem that the internal-mechanism literature exposes. Wallace et al. 2019 ("Do NLP Models Know Numbers? Probing Numeracy in Embeddings") showed that token/word [[Concept - Embeddings as Learned Representations|embeddings]] *do* capture rough magnitude — a probe can recover a number's approximate value from its embedding — but the recovered values **extrapolate poorly beyond the training range**: magnitude is encoded, but not in a way that composes or generalizes. And when models *do* compute, they often implement addition with **Fourier/trigonometric features on digit embeddings** — the same mechanism reverse-engineered in [[Concept - Grokking]]'s modular-arithmetic circuit (Nanda et al. 2023), and echoed in more recent work finding LLMs represent numbers on a helix and add via trig identities (Kantamneni & Tegmark 2024 — *folklore-adjacent but real*: a specific, testable mechanistic claim). The point: the model builds a value-aware internal geometry *despite* the tokenizer, and the tokenizer's job is to not sabotage it.

## In practice
The labs converged on a small set of fixes — all of them [[Concept - Tokenizer Training]] decisions made before any weights exist — each a direct response to the merge-table pathology:
- **Single-digit tokenization** — PaLM and the LLaMA family tokenize each digit `0`–`9` as its own token, so *every* number is a compositional sequence of digit tokens and the per-place-value circuit can be reused. This is the blunt, reliable fix.
- **Fixed-size digit chunking** — Llama 3 groups digits into three-digit chunks; GPT-4's `cl100k_base` / `o200k` similarly reserve tokens for numbers `000`–`999`, tokenizing longer numbers into groups of up to three digits. Chunking shortens sequences (fewer tokens per number) at the cost of reintroducing some non-compositionality at chunk boundaries.
- **Right-to-left grouping** — split the number from the *units* end so the ones digit always lands at a fixed position relative to a chunk boundary. This aligns place values across different-length numbers, and **right-to-left grouping measurably improves addition accuracy** — a cheap preprocessing trick with no architecture change. (This is why some pipelines literally reverse the number before tokenizing.)

Beyond tokenization there are **value-aware encodings** that inject the magnitude directly:
- **xVal** (Golkar et al. 2023) uses a single `[NUM]` token whose embedding is *scaled by the number's actual value*, giving a continuous inductive bias — excellent for scientific/tabular data, at the cost of limited dynamic range.
- **Abacus embeddings** (McLeish et al. 2024, "Transformers Can Do Arithmetic with the Right Embeddings") add per-digit positional hints encoding each digit's place within its number; this unlocks dramatic **length generalization** — training addition on up to 20-digit numbers and generalizing to ~100-digit addition, which vanilla positional schemes cannot do.

For a practitioner, the operational rule is: for arithmetic-heavy, code, or finance workloads, **audit the tokenizer's digit handling before blaming the model**, and always test with *off-distribution digit lengths* — see [[Reference - Architecture Numerology]] for where digit/vocab conventions sit among the other magic constants, and pair numeric prompts with [[Concept - Chain-of-Thought and Why It Works]], which lets the model externalize per-digit steps it can't do in one forward pass.

## Failure modes
- **`9.11 > 9.9`.** The canonical demonstration. Two forces conspire: `9.11` and `9.9` tokenize differently (the fractional parts become different tokens with no shared place-value alignment), *and* the training distribution is full of contexts — software versions, dates, Bible verses (chapter.verse), book sections — where "9.11" legitimately comes *after* "9.9". The model has learned an ordering where 9.11 is "bigger," and tokenization removes the digit-alignment signal that would override it. Detection: probe decimal comparisons across many pairs; the errors cluster on pairs where the shorter number has the larger tenths digit.
- **"How many R's in `strawberry`?"** Character-level questions are hard because BPE *hides the character composition of tokens*: the model sees the token id for `straw` + `berry` (or similar), never the individual letters, so counting characters requires recovering information the input representation threw away. Same root cause: the tokenizer is an information bottleneck between the string and the model — a classic entry in [[Gotchas - Tokenizer Pathologies]].
- **Spooky per-value error patterns.** Because chunk boundaries depend on corpus frequency, arithmetic accuracy is **non-monotone in the specific digits**: `2017 + x` can behave differently from `2018 + x` purely because one addend is a single token and the other is two. This produces error maps that look like reasoning inconsistencies but are pure tokenization artifacts — a genuine trap when debugging a "flaky" arithmetic capability, and a cousin of the under-trained-id surprises catalogued in [[Lore - Glitch Tokens]].
- **Short-number accuracy over-promising.** A model that nails 3-digit addition can collapse on 12-digit addition because value-encoding extrapolates poorly (Wallace et al.). Never accept short-number benchmarks as evidence of numeric competence.

## The non-obvious
The unicorn insight is that **digit tokenization is a silent capability cliff, invisible in the model card.** Two models of identical parameter count and training compute can differ enormously on arithmetic *purely* because one tokenizes digits singly and the other merges them by frequency — and nothing in the benchmark suite will tell you which unless the suite deliberately includes off-distribution digit lengths. The corollary that catches teams: an arithmetic regression after swapping a base model can be entirely a tokenizer change, not a "smarter/dumber" model change, and the right first move is to diff the two tokenizers on a batch of numbers, not to fine-tune. And the deepest point: the tokenizer is an **information bottleneck placed before the model can reason at all** — it decides, upstream of every parameter, what compositional structure the network is even allowed to see. Fixing numeracy is therefore often a *pre-model* decision (single-digit or right-to-left tokenization, value-aware embeddings) that dominates anything you can do with more scale — a rare case where the cheapest lever is also the strongest.
