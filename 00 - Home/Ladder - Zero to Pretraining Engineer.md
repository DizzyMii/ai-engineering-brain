# Ladder - Zero to Pretraining Engineer

A guided walk through the Engineering Wing for one question: *what do you actually need to know to plan, launch, and babysit a large pretraining run?* Thirty-five steps, ordered so the story builds: the numerical and optimization substrate first, then the Transformer itself, then the systems and data engineering that scale it past one GPU, then the stability tricks and frontier techniques that keep a nine-figure run from diverging, closing on the unicorn tier — the incidents every pretraining team eventually lives through. Read each note for the one mechanism or number named under it; if you can answer the self-test without re-reading, you've got the point and can move on — if you can't, that's the note to actually sit with. Treat the recurring numbers as things to memorize rather than look up mid-run: 6ND, 16-20 bytes/param, ~20 tokens/param, and 40-55% MFU are the arithmetic a real pretraining plan is built from. This ladder draws on domains 01, 02, 03, 04, 05, and 08, plus one closing entry from 19; for the full 25-domain map, including the Applied Wing where these models eventually land and earn out, start at [[Home]].

## Act I — The Substrate (numerical and mathematical foundations)

**1. [[Concept - Entropy and Cross-Entropy]]**
Extract the identity that makes "the loss went from 2.11 to 2.07" physically meaningful: cross-entropy = entropy + KL divergence, so training on cross-entropy loss is literally minimizing the model's distance from the true data distribution, and the gradient of softmax-plus-cross-entropy is the clean $\text{softmax}(z) - \text{onehot}$ every framework relies on. Perplexity is the exponential of that loss — the model's effective branching factor — and Chinchilla's fitted irreducible term, $E \approx 1.69$ nats/token, is the empirical floor loss curves flatten toward. The trap: perplexity is measured per token, so comparing it across two different tokenizers is meaningless unless you renormalize to bits-per-byte first.
*Self-test:* A model's perplexity looks worse after you swap in a bigger-vocabulary tokenizer, even though its downstream benchmarks improved — what's actually going on?

**2. [[Concept - Floating Point for Deep Learning]]**
Extract the one tradeoff that explains the entire modern precision stack: exponent bits buy dynamic range, mantissa bits buy relative precision, and deep-learning gradients — spanning ten orders of magnitude but tolerating rounding noise — want range over precision. That's why bf16 kept fp32's full 8-bit exponent (the same ~$10^{38}$ range) and sacrificed mantissa bits down to 7, while fp16's 10 mantissa bits but 5-bit exponent (max value 65504) is why it needed a whole loss-scaling apparatus to survive training at all. Matmul accumulation error grows like $\sqrt{k}\,\varepsilon$, which is why tensor cores always accumulate dot products in fp32 regardless of what precision the inputs are stored in.
*Self-test:* Why does bf16 never need loss scaling the way fp16 does, even though bf16 has fewer mantissa bits?

**3. [[Concept - Matrix Multiplication as the Atom of Deep Learning]]**
Extract the FLOP-counting rule every compute budget in this field is built on: a dense layer costs $2mnk$ FLOPs, and because backprop runs two GEMMs for every one forward GEMM, a transformer costs **~6 FLOPs per parameter per token** — 2 forward, 4 backward — giving the $C \approx 6ND$ identity underneath scaling laws. The second number to keep: arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte moved) decides whether an operation is compute-bound or memory-bound, and a batch-1 decode matmul runs at roughly **0.3% of peak FLOPs** on an H100 purely because of its shape — same hardware, 300x apart, on shape alone.
*Self-test:* Why does cutting the FLOP count of a memory-bound matmul, like an LLM decode step, save you nothing in wall-clock time?

## Act II — How a Network Learns (training mechanics)

**4. [[Concept - Backpropagation]]**
Extract the absurdly favorable exchange rate that makes deep learning economically possible: one backward pass differentiates a single scalar loss with respect to billions of parameters at a cost independent of parameter count — roughly 2x the forward pass, always. The hidden cost is memory, not FLOPs: every forward activation must stay alive until its gradient is consumed, which is why activation (gradient) checkpointing — store every $\sqrt{L}$-th layer, recompute the rest — trades ~33% more compute for memory that drops from $O(L)$ to $O(\sqrt{L})$, the same recompute-over-store logic FlashAttention later applies to the attention matrix itself.
*Self-test:* Why is backprop's cost independent of how many parameters a model has, and what does that imply is the real binding constraint at scale?

**5. [[Concept - The Training Loop]]**
Extract the ordering contract that most "model" bugs actually violate: zero_grad → forward → backward → (unscale) → clip → step → scheduler.step(), where every permutation is a silent, error-free failure mode rather than a crash. The load-bearing fact is that backward *accumulates* gradients with `+=` by design — forget `zero_grad` and the effective gradient norm grows every step until the run stalls or explodes, which is exactly the mechanism [[Concept - Gradient Accumulation and Microbatching]] exploits on purpose. Effective batch size is $B_\text{micro} \times N_\text{accum} \times W_\text{data-parallel}$ — the quantity optimization actually responds to, independent of how it's sharded across GPUs.
*Self-test:* A training run's gradient norm grows monotonically every step until the loss explodes — what's the single most likely ordering bug, and why does the framework never raise an error for it?

**6. [[Concept - Adam and AdamW]]**
Extract why "Adam+L2" and "AdamW" are not the same optimizer at matched hyperparameters: L2 regularization adds $\lambda\theta$ to the gradient, so under Adam it gets divided by $\sqrt{\hat v}$ and shrinks less exactly where gradient history is largest, inverting the regularizer's intent — AdamW decouples decay from the adaptive machinery entirely. The memory number to internalize: Adam's fp32 moments cost **8 bytes/param** on top of weights and gradients, making optimizer state the single largest pool in training memory and the first thing [[Concept - Data Parallelism and ZeRO|ZeRO]] shards. LLM pretraining almost universally uses $\beta_2 = 0.95$ instead of the textbook 0.999, because at large batch the gradient estimate is already low-variance and a shorter second-moment memory adapts to curvature shifts faster.
*Self-test:* Why does lowering AdamW's beta2 from 0.999 to 0.95 make a large training run less prone to loss spikes?

**7. [[Concept - RMSNorm and LayerNorm]]**
Extract why RMSNorm displaced LayerNorm almost everywhere by 2026: dropping mean-centering and the bias term costs nothing in quality but buys a **7-64% op-level speedup** (Zhang & Sennrich 2019), because rescaling to unit RMS turns out to be the load-bearing half of the operation. The non-obvious mechanical point: a norm layer's backward pass orthogonalizes the upstream gradient against the normalized activation direction, filtering out exactly the component that would change magnitude — part of why norms are such an effective fix for vanishing and exploding gradients. One hard rule: the sum-of-squares reduction must run in **fp32**, never natively in bf16/fp16, or the variance computation silently corrupts and the run diverges thousands of steps later with no crash to point to.
*Self-test:* RMSNorm drops LayerNorm's mean-subtraction step entirely — what does the paper's own evidence say that step was actually buying you?

**8. [[Concept - Weight Initialization]]**
Extract the one-line derivation behind every named init scheme: forward signal variance is preserved only if $\text{fan\_in} \cdot \sigma_W^2 = 1$, and because that factor compounds across $L$ layers, a per-layer error of even 10% is the difference between $0.9^{100} \approx 2.7\times10^{-5}$ (dead network) and $1.1^{100} \approx 1.4\times10^4$ (blown up). Transformers add a second, additive problem: each of $N$ layers writes twice into the residual stream, so GPT-2's $1/\sqrt{2N}$ scaling on the residual-projection init exists purely to stop that stream's variance from growing linearly with depth — a correction every modern LLM config still carries even though every block is now drenched in RMSNorm.
*Self-test:* Normalization can absorb a 2-3x initialization error in the forward pass — so why does the $1/\sqrt{2N}$ residual-projection scaling survive in every modern LLM config anyway?

**9. [[Concept - Vanishing and Exploding Gradients]]**
Extract the multiplicative mechanism underneath: backprop's gradient at layer 0 is a product of per-layer Jacobians, so a per-layer spectral norm of just 0.9 or 1.1 compounds to $2.7\times10^{-5}$ or $1.4\times10^4$ by depth 100 — nothing is wrong with any single layer, the pathology is emergent from the product. Residual connections are the strongest fix because they change each factor to $I + F'$, an additive identity term the product cannot kill. The transformer-specific non-obvious point: exploding gradients usually enter through **attention logits**, not the FFN stack classical theory worries about — which is why the effective fixes are logit-level (QK-norm, z-loss) rather than another init tweak.
*Self-test:* A 30-layer transformer NaNs at step 8,000, right at the end of warmup — classical theory says check the init constants, but where should you actually look first, and why?

## Act III — The Architecture

**10. [[Deep Dive - The Transformer]]**
Extract the skeleton every frontier LLM shares: a stack of identical pre-norm residual blocks, each reading from and writing additively to a shared `d_model`-wide **residual stream** — attention and the FFN are read/write operations on a bus, not sequential transformations. The parameter-count approximation $N_{\text{params}} \approx 12 \cdot n_{\text{layers}} \cdot d_{\text{model}}^2$ falls straight out of `d_ff = 4*d_model` and four attention projections — it matched GPT-3's 175B almost exactly, but is 30-90% off for any GQA or MoE model. The single efficiency property that made transformers win over RNNs: causal masking lets the entire sequence's next-token loss compute in **one parallel forward pass**, with no sequential dependency during training.
*Self-test:* The `12*n_layers*d_model^2` parameter formula matches GPT-3 almost exactly — why does it fail badly the moment you apply it to a GQA or MoE model?

**11. [[Concept - Feed-Forward Networks and GLU Variants]]**
Extract the sizing fact that matters for budgeting: the FFN sublayer holds roughly **two-thirds** of a transformer block's parameters and FLOPs, which is exactly why [[Concept - Mixture of Experts Architecture]] sparsifies the FFN and never the attention sublayer. SwiGLU and its GLU-family relatives add a third weight matrix — an elementwise gate — and consistently beat plain GELU/ReLU FFNs at matched compute; to keep parameter count comparable to a vanilla 4x FFN, LLaMA shrinks $d_{ff}$ to roughly $\tfrac{8}{3} d_{model}$. The honest caveat worth remembering: Shazeer's own 2020 paper offers **no theoretical explanation** for why SwiGLU wins — a one-page empirical ablation became a load-bearing default across nearly every open-weight model since.
*Self-test:* If you're porting inference code for a LLaMA-family checkpoint and hardcode the vanilla `d_ff = 4*d_model` convention, what kind of failure do you get — loud or silent?

**12. [[Concept - Normalization Placement (Pre-Norm, Post-Norm, DeepNorm)]]**
Extract why this one placement choice decides whether a deep transformer trains at all: post-norm (`Norm(x + Sublayer(x))`) renormalizes every layer's output but forces gradients back through every intervening norm, becoming unstable past roughly 20-30 layers without careful warmup; pre-norm (`x + Sublayer(Norm(x))`) gives gradients a clean, unnormalized highway to early layers but lets the residual stream's variance grow unboundedly with depth, causing late-layer **representation collapse** (Liu et al. 2020). DeepNorm's fix — upscale the residual by $\alpha > 1$, downscale sublayer init by $\beta < 1$ — trained a 1000-layer transformer where neither vanilla version could. The field hasn't fully converged: Gemma 2's 2024 return to sandwich norm plus QK-norm is a scale-tested admission that pure pre-norm's "case closed" narrative was premature.
*Self-test:* Pre-norm transformers train more stably than post-norm ones at depth — so why did Gemma 2 partially reverse course back toward post-norm-style sandwich normalization in 2024?

## Act IV — Scale (why one GPU isn't enough, and what replaces it)

**13. [[Concept - Why Models Don't Fit on One GPU]]**
Extract the sizing mistake that catches every junior engineer: training memory has four pools — parameters (2 bytes bf16), gradients (2-4 bytes), Adam optimizer state (12 bytes fp32), and activations — summing to roughly **16-20 bytes per parameter** before a single activation is stored, meaning a 7B model needs 112-140GB, already past an 80GB H100 with zero activations counted. The practical ceiling on one GPU without sharding is roughly **3-6B dense parameters**; past that you must shard state (ZeRO/FSDP) or shard the model itself (tensor/pipeline parallelism) — there is no clever kernel that makes 70B+ dense pretraining fit on one device.
*Self-test:* A 7B model's bf16 weights are only 14GB — so why does the actual training job need well over 100GB before storing a single activation?

**14. [[Concept - Scaling Laws]]**
Extract the correction that reset the field: Kaplan et al. (2020) said scale parameters faster than data; Hoffmann et al.'s Chinchilla (2022) re-ran the fit with IsoFLOP profiles and found the earlier conclusion was a methodology artifact (an under-tuned LR schedule) — the corrected compute-optimal ratio is **roughly 20 tokens per parameter**, and a 4x-smaller Chinchilla beat 4x-larger Gopher at equal compute. The frontier has since moved past pure compute-optimality into deliberate **inference-optimal overtraining**: Llama-3 8B trained on ~15T tokens, roughly 1875 tokens/parameter, nearly 100x past Chinchilla-optimal, because serving cost tracks parameter count, not training tokens.
*Self-test:* Two models have identical pretraining loss — what does this note say you still can't conclude about how they'll compare on any single downstream benchmark?

**15. [[Concept - Data Parallelism and ZeRO]]**
Extract the insight ZeRO is built on: plain data parallelism replicates the full model on every rank, buying throughput but zero extra capacity — a 70B model that doesn't fit on one GPU still doesn't fit under DDP no matter how many GPUs you add, because every rank pays the full 16-bytes/param bill regardless of world size. ZeRO shards that redundant state in three stages — optimizer states (ZeRO-1), + gradients (ZeRO-2), + parameters (ZeRO-3, which all-gathers each layer just-in-time and costs ~1.5x DDP's communication volume). Concretely, a 70B model's 1.12TB Adam footprint shards to **~18GB/GPU** across 64 ranks under ZeRO-3 — but the non-obvious trap is reaching for ZeRO-3 reflexively at the first OOM, when ZeRO-1 often already fits the model at a fraction of the communication cost.
*Self-test:* A team hits an OOM and jumps straight to ZeRO-3, but throughput actually drops — what's the most likely diagnostic mistake they made?

**16. [[Concept - Tensor and Pipeline Parallelism]]**
Extract the two orthogonal ways to split a model too big for state-sharding alone: tensor parallelism cuts individual matmuls across GPUs (Megatron's column-parallel-then-row-parallel split needs exactly **4 all-reduces per transformer layer**) and must stay inside a single NVLink domain because it can't tolerate inter-node bandwidth at that frequency; pipeline parallelism instead puts different *layers* on different GPUs, paying an idle **bubble** of $(p-1)/(m+p-1)$ — at $p=8$ stages and only $m=8$ microbatches that's a 47% idle fraction, which is why $m \geq 4p$ is the rule of thumb. Production layouts commonly run **TP=8** (one full node) composed with **PP=8-16** across nodes, layered under DP/ZeRO as the outermost dimension.
*Self-test:* Why must tensor parallelism stay confined inside a single node's NVLink domain, while pipeline parallelism can safely cross nodes?

**17. [[Concept - Mixed Precision Training]]**
Extract the master-weight discipline that keeps bf16 training correct: even though bf16 never overflows the way fp16 does, a relative update smaller than about **0.8%** of a weight's magnitude rounds to exactly zero if applied directly in bf16 — late in training, with a decayed LR, per-step updates routinely fall below that threshold, so the optimizer keeps an **fp32 master copy** and only rounds down to bf16 for the next forward pass. A short fixed list stays in fp32 unconditionally regardless of model dtype: the loss, softmax, norm statistics, and the gradient all-reduce. The dangerous asymmetry: fp16 failure announces itself (a shrinking loss-scale factor, skipped steps); bf16 divergence shows up as a run that looks fine for 40,000 steps and then quietly starts producing slightly-too-high loss with no single event to root-cause.
*Self-test:* A training run in bf16 has been going for 40,000 steps and looks healthy, but the loss curve is now a hair above an fp32 reference run — what class of bug should you suspect before touching hyperparameters?

**18. [[Concept - Learning Rate Schedules for Pretraining]]**
Extract why warmup exists mechanically, not just as folklore: Adam's second-moment estimate $\hat v$ starts at zero and is uncalibrated for the first thousands of steps, so jumping straight to peak LR is enough to spike a run before curvature is characterized. Cosine decay anneals to ~10% of peak over the *entire planned token budget* — but that means $T$ must be fixed in advance, and stopping early leaves you at an artificially high LR with no way to recover the lost decay. **Warmup-stable-decay (WSD)** sidesteps that commitment with a long constant-LR plateau you can checkpoint-branch from at any point — its real advantage isn't a better final loss, it's optionality.
*Self-test:* A cosine-scheduled run gets cut short at 70% of its planned token budget — why is the resulting checkpoint's loss artificially inflated relative to what more training would have bought, in a way a WSD schedule's checkpoint wouldn't be?

**19. [[Concept - Tokenizer Training]]**
Extract the decision that's frozen for a model's entire life the moment pretraining starts: nearly every frontier model trains its tokenizer with greedy [[Concept - Byte-Pair Encoding]] — repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent symbol pair, frequency-greedy rather than likelihood-optimal, unlike SentencePiece's EM-pruned unigram-LM alternative. Vocabulary size is a real tradeoff, not a free parameter: common operating points run 32k (Llama-2) to 256k (Gemma), and a tokenizer trained mostly on English text imposes a **2-4x token tax** (fertility) on other languages because the merge statistics never saw them in proportion. The irreversibility is the sting: because the embedding matrix's row count is fixed, there's no "add more vocabulary later" — a tokenizer bug caught after a multi-million-dollar run has started is a decision to live with it or restart.
*Self-test:* Why can't you simply add 10,000 new vocabulary entries to a model's tokenizer partway through pretraining the way you could add more training data?

**20. [[Deep Dive - Anatomy of a Pretraining Run]]**
Extract the wiring diagram tying the whole domain together: a compute budget $C$ solves $6ND = C$ for parameter count and token count, which fixes the parallelism layout needed to hit the field's target **40-55% MFU** band — Llama-3 405B's reported 54-day/16k-H100 run backs out to roughly **49% MFU**, a useful sanity check that a claimed run is physically plausible. The mechanism worth internalizing over the ML recipe: at 10,000+ GPU scale, **hardware failure is the steady state, not a contingency** — elastic restart from checkpoint is part of the normal operating loop, and most engineering hours go to checkpoint cadence, straggler detection, and data-pipeline resumability rather than architecture or hyperparameters.
*Self-test:* A lab reports a headline MFU number for their pretraining run — what does that number's denominator typically NOT include, and why does that make the true "useful compute per wall-clock hour" look better than reality?

## Act V — Data (the pipeline that feeds the run)

**21. [[Deep Dive - The Pretraining Data Pipeline]]**
Extract the ordering logic that is itself the pipeline's cost-optimization strategy: language ID must run first (filters are language-specific), **deduplication must run before quality scoring** because scoring is the expensive stage and a 30-50%-duplicate raw pool means paying classifier cost on the same page ten times over, and decontamination runs last against a benchmark list that keeps growing. End-to-end survival rate — final tokens over raw input tokens — typically lands at **1-15%**; FineWeb kept 15T final tokens out of 96 processed Common Crawl dumps. The competitive point: the transformer architecture and training loop are public knowledge reproducible from papers, but exact filter thresholds, dedup granularity, and mixture weights are the part frontier labs say the least about, because it's the part that isn't.
*Self-test:* Two labs start from the same raw Common Crawl dumps and end up with measurably different models — according to this note, where did that difference most likely get manufactured?

**22. [[Concept - Deduplication at Scale]]**
Extract the MinHash-LSH mechanism that turns an $O(n^2)$ similarity problem into fixed-size fingerprint comparisons: shingle each document, keep the minimum hash under $k$ permutations as a signature (matching-coordinate fraction estimates Jaccard similarity), then band the signature so candidate pairs emerge above a similarity threshold set by $(b,r)$. The real number: SlimPajama's dedup of RedPajama cut **1.2T tokens down to 627B — 49.6% removed**, meaning half a raw web corpus is duplicate content by volume. The sharpest non-obvious finding: FineWeb showed **global cross-dump dedup can make a corpus worse**, because high-quality content gets re-syndicated and mirrored more, not less — the fix was deduping within each dump instead of across all of them.
*Self-test:* FineWeb found that deduplicating more aggressively across dumps actually hurt corpus quality — why does "more duplication removed" not mean "better corpus" here?

**23. [[Concept - Data Mixtures]]**
Extract the mechanism behind "the model saw Wikipedia N times": a source's sampling weight combined with its raw size sets its effective-epoch count, so a small high-quality domain like Wikipedia gets deliberately upsampled to 2-5x effective epochs while the much larger, noisier web crawl is downsampled under one pass. The most counterintuitive, now-measured effect: **adding source code to the mix improves non-code reasoning** — a genuine cross-domain transfer effect, which is why labs add code even when they don't want a coding model. Mixture weight isn't fixed across training either: the final 10-20% typically runs an **annealing/cooldown phase** that shifts toward the highest-quality, code, and synthetic data, timed against the LR decay.
*Self-test:* Why does adding source code to a pretraining mixture measurably improve a model's performance on reasoning benchmarks that have nothing to do with code?

**24. [[Concept - Quality Filtering for Pretraining Data]]**
Extract the three-paradigm cost ladder: cheap heuristic rules (length, symbol-to-word ratio) catch the easy half of the junk for nearly free; perplexity filters score documents against a reference language model; classifier/LLM-annotator filters — FineWeb-Edu prompts Llama-3-70B-Instruct to rate educational value, then distills that into a cheap linear classifier — go furthest and produced a measured **5-7 point jump on MMLU and ARC** at matched token count. Survival rate is the health signal: above ~50% your filters are too weak, well under 0.5% you're throwing away usable text. The canonical failure mode is [[Lore - The C4 Blocklist Incident]]: a lexical "bad words" blocklist disproportionately deleted LGBTQ+ content and African-American-aligned English while unrelated harmful content survived — every filter is a value judgment, not a neutral sieve.
*Self-test:* A quality filter's survival rate is 80% — is that more likely a sign of a healthy pipeline or a broken one, and why?

## Act VI — The Cluster (the physical machine underneath)

**25. [[Concept - Anatomy of an AI Training Cluster]]**
Extract the bandwidth cliff that shapes every distributed training decision: inside an 8-GPU node, NVSwitch gives every GPU **~900 GB/s** to every other GPU, all-to-all; the instant you leave the node, InfiniBand/RoCE drops that to **~50 GB/s per GPU** — a 10-18x cliff that is the reason tensor parallelism stays pinned inside one node while data/pipeline parallelism is pushed across the slower fabric. Scale realities as of 2026: production clusters run to roughly 100,000 GPUs, and power/cooling — not floor space — is the binding physical constraint, with NVIDIA's rack-scale GB200 systems pushing **~120kW/rack**, forcing a wholesale move to liquid cooling.
*Self-test:* Why is tensor parallelism confined to a single node's NVLink domain while data parallelism routinely spans the entire cluster?

**26. [[Concept - GPU Interconnects (NVLink, InfiniBand, RoCE)]]**
Extract the three-tier bandwidth hierarchy in real numbers: NVLink4 gives ~900 GB/s aggregate per GPU inside a node via NVSwitch; PCIe Gen5 tops out around **64 GB/s — 14x slower** — the path traffic falls onto without NVSwitch or during host-staged transfers; InfiniBand NDR or RoCEv2 deliver roughly 50 GB/s per GPU node-to-node. GPUDirect RDMA is what makes either scale-out fabric fast at all, letting the NIC read/write GPU HBM directly instead of bouncing through host memory. RoCE's specific failure mode is worth knowing by name: without correctly tuned Priority Flow Control and ECN, an incast pattern — common in MoE all-to-all dispatch — triggers a **PFC pause-storm** that cascades backward and can stall an entire rail.
*Self-test:* A single-pair NCCL micro-benchmark between two GPUs shows near-peak bandwidth, but the production 512-GPU training run is badly communication-bound — why doesn't the micro-benchmark catch this?

**27. [[Concept - All-Reduce and Collective Operations]]**
Extract the cost model every distributed step pays: $T \approx \alpha \cdot \text{steps} + \text{bytes}/\text{bandwidth}$, where ring all-reduce is bandwidth-optimal — it moves roughly $2\times$ the data **independent of GPU count $N$** — but its $2(N-1)$-step latency term is easy to misread as free at scale: at 8 GPUs those 14 steps are noise, at 512 GPUs the 1022 steps can eclipse the bandwidth term entirely, which is why production frameworks switch to tree or hierarchical collectives past a certain size. Hierarchical collectives exploit the two-tier interconnect directly: reduce inside the fast NVLink domain first, then run one slow cross-node collective between node representatives. All-to-all — the collective behind MoE expert dispatch — is the one practitioners fear most, because its cost is sensitive to load imbalance across experts.
*Self-test:* Ring all-reduce is described as "bandwidth-optimal, independent of N" — so in what precise sense does its cost still grow as you add more GPUs to the ring?

**28. [[Concept - Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU)]]**
Extract why MFU is the single number infrastructure teams treat as a KPI worth dedicated headcount: two teams with identical clusters training the same model can differ 2x in wall-clock time purely on engineering quality, because $\text{MFU} = \text{achieved model FLOP/s} / \text{peak hardware FLOP/s}$ converts directly into cost — going from 40% to 55% MFU cuts training cost by roughly **27% with zero change to the model**. PaLM's **46.2% MFU** on TPU v4 pods is the reference benchmark; well-tuned H100 runs reach 50-60%. Know the denominator trap: MFU vs. HFU differ in whether activation-recomputation FLOPs count as "useful," and vendor peak-FLOPs numbers are sometimes quoted at sparsity-inflated or boost-clock rates.
*Self-test:* Two labs both report "MFU of 50%" for similar training runs — name one reason those two numbers might not actually be comparable.

## Act VII — Stability and the Frontier (keeping a nine-figure run alive)

**29. [[Concept - Training Stability and Loss Spikes]]**
Extract the mechanistic chain behind a spike: attention logits grow, softmax saturates toward near-one-hot, its gradient becomes a delta function that backprops into a large correlated update, and Adam's second-moment estimate $\hat v$ — which lags a sudden gradient-magnitude jump by design — briefly under-normalizes that update instead of damping it. Every named stabilizer attacks one link in that chain: QK-norm caps attention-logit growth directly, z-loss discourages the output softmax normalizer from drifting, beta2=0.95 shortens how long $\hat v$ stays stale. PaLM's sharpest finding: rewinding to before a spike and **replaying the identical data did not reproduce it** — the spike is an interaction between optimizer state and data *ordering*, not a property of the data alone.
*Self-test:* PaLM's team found that replaying the exact same data after a rewind did NOT reproduce a loss spike, but skipping the batches around it and continuing did — what does that rule out as the cause?

**30. [[Concept - muP and Hyperparameter Transfer]]**
Extract the problem muP solves and how expensive it is to get wrong: under standard parametrization, the optimal learning rate keeps shrinking as a transformer gets wider — wide-enough networks drift toward the NTK/lazy-training limit where only a fixed random feature map effectively learns — so every new model size traditionally needs its own multi-million-dollar sweep. muP's per-layer-type init and LR multipliers keep every layer in the maximal-feature-learning regime instead, enabling **muTransfer**: Yang et al. swept hyperparameters on a 40M-parameter proxy and transferred them, unchanged, to a 6.7B target near-optimally. The trap: a partially correct muP implementation doesn't crash — it silently trains to a plausible-looking loss curve at a suboptimal transferred LR, indistinguishable from an unlucky large-scale run unless you run the **coordinate check** first.
*Self-test:* A team implements muP's init-variance rules but forgets the matching per-layer LR multipliers — what happens when they train the model, and how would they actually catch the bug?

**31. [[Concept - FP8 Training]]**
Extract why fp8 pretraining spent years as "theoretically attractive, practically unstable" folklore before DeepSeek-V3: fp8's dynamic range is minuscule (roughly $2^{-9}$ to 448 for E4M3) compared to bf16's ~$10^{\pm38}$, so *scaling* — the per-tensor multiplier mapping real values into that tiny range — becomes the entire problem, not the 8-bit format itself. DeepSeek-V3's fix was **fine-grained scaling**: a separate scale per 1×128 tile for activations and 128×128 block for weights, so one outlier near an attention or MoE-router logit blows the scale for its own small tile instead of degrading the whole tensor — plus explicitly promoting fp8 matmul partial sums to fp32 accumulation. The proof point: 671B total parameters, ~2.788M H800 GPU-hours, the first production-scale fp8-pretrained frontier model, at quality close to a bf16 baseline.
*Self-test:* Early attempts at fp8 pretraining failed even though the E4M3/E5M2 formats were well-specified — what was the actual problem, if it wasn't the format itself?

**32. [[Concept - Critical Batch Size]]**
Extract the threshold that separates "more GPUs helps" from "more GPUs just burns compute": the gradient noise scale $B_\text{noise} \approx \text{tr}(H\Sigma)/g^\top H g$ marks the batch size below which doubling batch size linearly halves the steps needed to converge, and above which returns diminish sharply. Because $B_\text{noise}$ **grows as the loss falls**, frontier labs treat batch size as a schedule rather than a constant: GPT-3 ramped from 32k to **3.2M tokens** over training, tracking the rising threshold instead of picking one compromise value. The organizational trap: a large cluster reserved regardless of model size creates pressure to run above critical batch just to keep every GPU busy — the run shows excellent MFU while converging no faster, in wall-clock terms, than a smaller batch would have.
*Self-test:* A training run shows near-peak MFU and every GPU looks fully utilized — what additional piece of evidence would tell you the batch size is actually wasting compute above the critical batch?

## The unicorn tier — the war stories

**33. [[Lore - The Loss Spike Chronicles]]**
Extract the framing that makes this note different from the mechanism note it dramatizes: every stabilizer in a modern pretraining recipe — QK-norm, z-loss, beta2=0.95, bf16 over fp16 — is literally **scar tissue** from one of four publicly documented 2022 runs. OPT-175B fought fp16 loss-scale collapse with pure manual triage (rewind, lower LR, resume); PaLM documented ~20 spikes and found replaying identical data after a rewind did not reproduce one, but skipping the surrounding batches did; GLM-130B traced spikes to embedding-gradient growth; BLOOM switched to bf16 specifically to dodge the crisis OPT had just lived through. The fifth actor hiding in every one of these logs: a single-rank NaN that looks like an optimization spike is often just [[Lore - Silent Data Corruption at Scale|silent data corruption]] on one GPU — the first fork in any real triage session.
*Self-test:* PaLM's team could not get a loss spike to recur by replaying the exact same data — what does that finding rule in as the real cause, mechanically, in terms of Adam's internal state?

**34. [[Lore - Silent Data Corruption at Scale]]**
Extract the failure mode that fires no alarm at all: "mercurial cores" silently compute wrong answers on specific operand patterns — no crash, no ECC event, no exception — discovered independently by Meta (Dixit et al. 2021) and Google (Hochschild et al. 2021) at a rate of **a few bad cores per several thousand machines**. For a pretraining run this isn't abstract: it shows up as a loss spike that reproduces on one specific rank and vanishes when the job moves to different hardware, or worse, gradient corruption that never trips a NaN check and silently poisons a checkpoint you keep training from. Detection requires abandoning the assumption that hardware is a trustworthy oracle: deterministic replay on a second GPU, per-rank grad-norm outlier monitoring, and checksums around collectives — see the fuller [[Gotchas - Hardware Failures at Scale|hardware-failure taxonomy]], where SDC ranks as the single most dangerous entry precisely because nothing fires.
*Self-test:* A loss spike disappears when you move the training job to different hardware but the data and code are unchanged — what does that specific behavior tell you about the cause?

**35. [[Lore - The OPT-175B Logbook]]**
Extract the closing case study: in May 2022, Meta published not just OPT-175B's weights but its actual `chronicles` logbook — the daily, unsanitized record of training a 175B model on 992 A100s over two months, including dozens of checkpoint rollbacks, most of them triggered by **hardware**, not the model (dead GPUs, uncorrectable ECC errors, NCCL hangs), plus a running fight against fp16 loss-scale collapse. The load-bearing engineering lesson stated as a formula: work lost per failure equals steps since the last checkpoint, which makes **checkpoint cadence the actual cost dial** of the whole operation. The second-order lesson is about transparency as infrastructure: publishing the logbook converted private tribal knowledge into a shared baseline, and BLOOM formalized the same firefighting two months later, switching to bf16 specifically to escape the fp16 crisis OPT had just lived through.
*Self-test:* Susan Zhang's team restarted OPT-175B's training dozens of times — what fraction of the underlying causes were actually about the model or optimizer, versus something else entirely, and what does that imply about where pretraining engineering effort should go?
