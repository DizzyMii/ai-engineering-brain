# Ladder - Zero to Post-Training Engineer

A guided walk through post-training (domain 06), the stage that turns a raw next-token predictor into a model that follows instructions, prefers good answers over bad ones, and reasons through verifiable problems. It's for anyone who wants to go from knowing what RLHF stands for to running an SFT/RLHF/RLVR pipeline and knowing why it broke at 3 a.m. Twenty-nine steps, each building on the last. First the two information-theoretic quantities every loss in this track reduces to. Then the pipeline's shape and its unglamorous data mechanics, preference learning from reward models through PPO to DPO and its variants, and verifiable-reward RL and reasoning training. Then how you'd measure whether any of it worked, and finally the named ways each stage fails (reward hacking, entropy collapse, length bias, mode collapse), roughly in order of how much pain each causes. Read each note for the one thing named under it. If you can answer the self-test, move on. This ladder assumes a base model already exists. For the pretraining run that produced it, and the full 25-domain map, start at [[Home]].

## Act I — The two numbers every loss in this track reduces to

**1. [[Concept - Entropy and Cross-Entropy]]**
Every loss curve in this track is plotting one identity: $H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{KL}(p\|q)$. Cross-entropy is entropy plus the KL gap between the true data distribution and the model, so minimizing cross-entropy during SFT is minimizing forward KL divergence. With one-hot targets, cross-entropy reduces to negative log-likelihood, and paired with softmax it gives the clean gradient $\hat y - y$ that every stage from pretraining through SFT relies on. Treat perplexity as the exchange rate: a model at 20 ppl is, per token, as uncertain as a fair 20-sided die. And remember Chinchilla's fitted irreducible loss floor of ≈1.69 nats/token. No loss curve beats it, because it's a property of the data and not the model.
*Self-test:* Why does a language model's cross-entropy loss floor out above zero even for a perfectly trained model, and what does grinding against that floor tell you to go fix?

**2. [[Concept - KL Divergence]]**
The asymmetry here explains half the behavior in this track. Forward KL is mode-covering (pretraining/MLE forces the model to cover every data mode). Reverse KL is mode-seeking: the RLHF policy objective maximizes reward minus $\beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{ref})$, typical $\beta$ 0.01–0.1, and pushes the policy onto a narrow set of high-reward modes. An estimator detail recurs constantly downstream. The naive log-ratio estimator $k_1$ is unbiased but noisy and can go negative, so PPO and GRPO implementations default to Schulman's $k_3 = (r-1) - \log r$, which is unbiased, always non-negative, and lower-variance. If you only master one concept in the track, make it this one. DPO's whole derivation is an inversion of the closed-form solution to this KL-regularized objective.
*Self-test:* An RLHF dashboard shows reward climbing steadily while logged KL looks flat or even negative on some batches. Name two entirely different root causes that produce that same graph, and how you'd tell them apart.

**3. [[Concept - Softmax]]**
The numerical trap behind silent NaNs across this track: naive softmax overflows fp16 once logits exceed ≈11.09, and trained LMs routinely produce logits in the 10–30 range, so the max-subtracted stable form is mandatory. Softmax is shift-invariant. Logits have no absolute meaning, only differences within a row, so you can never compare raw logit magnitudes across checkpoints. The Jacobian result is what makes the post-training stack cheap to backprop through: composed with cross-entropy, softmax's gradient reduces to $\hat y - y$. The same sigmoid-flavored Bradley-Terry math shows up again verbatim inside reward models and DPO later in this ladder.
*Self-test:* Why can't you compare a token's raw logit value of 14.2 across two different checkpoints, and what does that mean vault-wide for interpretability tools that read logits directly?

**4. [[Concept - Loss Functions for Neural Networks]]**
Why cross-entropy and not MSE is the default classification loss: squared error through a sigmoid has gradient $(\hat y - y)\sigma'(z)$, and $\sigma'(z) \le 0.25$ decays to ~0 in the tails. A confidently wrong unit gets almost no gradient when it needs the most. CE cancels that saturating factor analytically. Keep the step-0 diagnostic handy: with balanced classes and random init, cross-entropy should sit at $\ln(C)$, which is $\ln(50257) \approx 10.82$ for GPT-2's vocabulary. Any deviation before you've touched the architecture is an init or label bug, not a training problem. The reduction (`mean` vs `sum` vs per-token) is a hidden learning-rate knob. Its most common production bug, dividing summed loss by all tokens instead of unmasked tokens, is what loss masking (three steps on) exists to prevent.
*Self-test:* A GPT-2-scale run starts with a step-0 loss of 14 instead of the expected ≈10.82. What two categories of bug does that number alone point to, before you've looked at a single generated token?

## Act II — The pipeline and its unglamorous mechanics

**5. [[Concept - The Post-Training Pipeline]]**
The canonical pipeline this ladder walks in order: SFT → preference optimization (RLHF or DPO) → optional RLVR on verifiable domains → distillation/merging. The headline number: InstructGPT's 1.3B RLHF'd model beat the raw 175B GPT-3 base model on human preference ratings. What a user experiences as "how good the model is" comes from post-training, not scale. Total post-training compute usually runs under 1–5% of the pretraining FLOPs that built the base checkpoint. That sets up the ladder's central reframe: post-training mostly *elicits and reweights* capability pretraining already installed instead of teaching new knowledge. LIMA found the same with roughly 1,000 curated examples.
*Self-test:* If post-training compute is 1–5% of pretraining compute, why does it have such an outsized effect on perceived model quality?

**6. [[Concept - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)]]**
SFT is unglamorous by design. It's the same training loop as pretraining, pointed at curated (prompt, response) demonstrations with loss masked to completion tokens only, typically 1–3 epochs at a learning rate of 1e-5–2e-5. That's well below pretraining LRs because the model already sits in a good region of the loss surface. LIMA's ~1,000-example result gave us the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis: quality and diversity of demonstrations matter far more than any hyperparameter. What makes this step matter downstream is that the SFT checkpoint becomes the frozen reference model every later KL-regularized stage is anchored to. A garbage or over-fit SFT policy passes its defects on however well later stages are tuned.
*Self-test:* Why does an over-fit or narrow SFT checkpoint damage every later RLHF/DPO stage, even if those stages are tuned perfectly?

**7. [[Concept - Loss Masking and Sequence Packing]]**
Two silent-failure mechanics decide whether an SFT run is correct. Loss masking sets `labels[i] = -100` on every non-assistant token so gradient only flows from completion tokens. A missing EOS in the unmasked labels means the model never learns to stop, which is invisible in training and only shows up as runaway generation at inference. Sequence packing removes the >50% padding waste from batching variable-length conversations. But without a block-diagonal "document" attention mask, later examples in a packed sequence attend across into earlier, unrelated ones. No crash, no metric shift, just a quality regression that's easy to blame on something else. Position IDs also have to reset per packed example, or RoPE's relative-position math is wrong past the first sub-sequence.
*Self-test:* An SFT run's loss curve looks normal and nothing crashes, but the model is measurably worse than an unpacked baseline. What do you check first, and why will the loss curve never show it?

**8. [[Concept - Chat Templates and Special Tokens]]**
This explains a whole category of "why did quality drop after we swapped models" incidents. A chat template isn't markup. It's the literal token sequence, with specific special tokens like `<|im_end|>` or `<|eot_id|>`, that the model saw during SFT, and each model family's template is incompatible with every other's. The most common silent bug is double-BOS: the template prepends one, the calling code prepends another, and the model gets a token sequence it never trained on without any exception. There's also a base-model trap. Applying an instruct model's chat template to a base checkpoint doesn't produce chat behavior, since the base model never learned to treat those tokens as structure. It just sees arbitrary text, which usually degrades the completion.
*Self-test:* You swap a served model's checkpoint but leave the old chat template in place. What does the service log or error out with, and how would you detect the regression?

## Act III — Preference learning: from reward models to DPO

**9. [[Concept - Reward Models]]**
The Bradley-Terry loss sits under every preference method in this track: $P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma(r_\theta(y_w) - r_\theta(y_l))$, trained as cross-entropy on the *difference* of two scalar scores. That makes the reward shift-invariant, so raw RM outputs have to be normalized before use. InstructGPT's RM was 6B parameters, initialized from the SFT checkpoint with its softmax head swapped for a single linear scalar output, and it ranked 4–9 completions per prompt to generate pairwise comparisons. The calibration ceiling: RewardBench pairwise accuracy runs roughly 65–75%. That isn't a training failure. Human-human label agreement sits in the same 65–75% band, and an RM can't be more reliable than the labels it learned from.
*Self-test:* An RM scores 90%+ pairwise accuracy on its own held-out training-distribution set. Why does that number alone tell you almost nothing about whether it's safe to optimize a policy against with RL?

**10. [[Deep Dive - RLHF End to End]]**
One resource fact explains why every successor in this ladder (DPO, GRPO) exists. RLHF's PPO stage keeps four full models in memory at once: trainable policy, frozen reference, frozen reward model, and a trained value/critic head. That's roughly 4x the memory of one model that size. The KL penalty against the frozen reference goes directly into the per-token reward instead of being a separate loss term, which gives PPO a dense signal in place of one scalar at the end of an 800-token sequence. Generation dominates wall-clock time because autoregressive decoding goes one token at a time, so production RLHF stacks run a fast inference engine like vLLM separately from the training process instead of idling one or the other.
*Self-test:* Name the four models in memory during RLHF's PPO stage and what each one is for. Then explain which two DPO eliminates and why that's possible.

**11. [[Concept - PPO for Language Models]]**
PPO is hard for language models because the reward is deferred and sparse. Every token gets near-zero reward except a per-token KL penalty, plus the reward model's scalar dropped once at the terminal EOS token, so assigning credit across hundreds of tokens is hard. The clipped surrogate objective bounds how far one gradient step can move the policy relative to the rollout-time policy ($\epsilon \approx 0.2$). Engstrom et al.'s "37 implementation details" finding matters more than the core algorithm: advantage whitening and reward normalization are required, and ablating them degrades PPO as much as changing the algorithm. The ratio clip and the KL penalty both bound "how far the policy moves," but they aren't redundant. The clip is a per-step brake and KL is the cumulative-drift budget. With the clip alone, a policy can wander arbitrarily far one legal small step at a time.
*Self-test:* PPO's ratio clip already bounds how much a single update can move the policy. So why isn't the separate KL-to-reference penalty redundant?

**12. [[Concept - KL Control in RLHF]]**
This note claims almost every RLHF postmortem traces back to one dial. $\beta$ in $\mathbb{E}[r] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{ref})$ trades reward directly against faithfulness to the reference. Gao et al.'s scaling-law study shows true reward rises then falls with KL, while the gold-vs-proxy gap grows roughly with $\sqrt{\text{KL}}$. Ziegler et al.'s adaptive KL controller turns picking $\beta$ from a one-shot guess into closed-loop control, adjusting proportionally to hit a target (InstructGPT-era runs commonly targeted ~6 nats). The counterintuitive lesson, stated plainly in the note: "just lower the KL penalty to let the model improve faster" is close to the most common way practitioners accidentally cause reward hacking. The fix for a run that looks stalled is almost never looser KL.
*Self-test:* Reward is climbing steadily and KL looks fine on the mean. Name two different things that could still be silently wrong, and the one number you'd check first to tell them apart.

**13. [[Concept - Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)]]**
The algebra that collapses RLHF's three stages into one. DPO inverts the closed-form KL-regularized optimum to express reward as $\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{ref})$ and substitutes that into the Bradley-Terry preference model. The intractable partition function $Z(x)$ cancels because it's identical for the chosen and rejected completions. What's left is a single classification-style loss computed from two log-probabilities: no reward model, no rollouts, no RL loop, at roughly SFT's compute cost. Typical $\beta \approx 0.1$ (range 0.01–0.5) is the same KL-strength knob as PPO's, baked into closed form instead of tuned online. The failure mode that matters most in practice: DPO's loss only constrains the *difference* between chosen and rejected log-probabilities, so both can fall together while the loss keeps improving. Llama 3's fix adds an NLL/SFT anchor term on the chosen response.
*Self-test:* A DPO run's loss looks healthy and the preference margin is growing, but outputs are getting less fluent. What two numbers do you need to log separately to catch this, and why does the margin alone hide it?

**14. [[Concept - The DPO Variant Family (IPO KTO ORPO SimPO)]]**
These four aren't competing reinventions. Each patches a separate DPO weakness. IPO swaps the log-sigmoid for a bounded squared loss so DPO's gradient stops pushing easily separable pairs to infinity. KTO reframes preference learning through prospect theory to use unpaired thumbs-up/down data, which DPO can't consume at all. ORPO drops the reference model entirely and folds an odds-ratio penalty into the SFT loss, so it's one training pass instead of two. SimPO also drops the reference but goes after length bias, length-normalizing the implicit reward ($\beta \approx 2$–2.5, $\gamma/\beta \approx 0.3$–0.5). Tulu 3's ablations show results are sensitive enough to dataset and $\beta$ that the loss function you pick matters less than whether you refresh the preference data on-policy between rounds. Teams hunting for the objectively best variant are optimizing the wrong axis.
*Self-test:* Your only preference signal is unpaired thumbs-up/thumbs-down on individual responses, with no matched counterfactual. Which of the four variants can you train on, and why can't plain DPO use this data at all?

## Act IV — Verifiable rewards and reasoning

**15. [[Concept - GRPO and RL with Verifiable Rewards]]**
GRPO makes one change to PPO. It samples a group of $G$ completions (typically 8–64) per prompt, scores each, and replaces the learned value network with the group's own mean and standard deviation as the baseline: $A_i = (r_i - \text{mean})/(\text{std}+\epsilon)$, broadcast identically to every token in that completion. Dropping the critic roughly halves the resident-model memory of full RLHF (policy + reference + reward, no critic), and that's what makes RL-scale reasoning training affordable. The sharpest edge case: if every sample in a group is right (or every one is wrong), the standard deviation is zero, every advantage is zero, and the group burns compute for no gradient. DAPO's dynamic sampling fixes this by resampling prompts until a group has a non-trivial pass rate.
*Self-test:* A GRPO group of 8 completions on a very easy prompt all get the right answer. What does the group-relative advantage come out to, and what does that cost you in wasted generation?

**16. [[Concept - Reasoning Training and Long Chain-of-Thought]]**
The result at the center of this rung was never engineered. The reward is accuracy plus a light format term, with no length term, yet response length grows monotonically over training, because longer deliberation correlates with getting hard problems right. Self-verification ("let me check this") and backtracking ("wait, that's wrong") emerge alongside it. The cheapest lever in the note is budget forcing: inject a token like "Wait" when the model tries to stop early, with zero extra training, and accuracy goes up directly. So the length the model settled on during RL was never a hard ceiling. Keep the generalization caveat in mind, since it sets up two steps later: Yue et al. found pass@k can be flat or worse after RL even as pass@1 rises, which is evidence for eliciting existing capability, not creating new reasoning.
*Self-test:* Nobody puts a length term in the reward, so why is response length the most-watched leading indicator of whether reasoning RL is "working," and why can a rising length curve lie to you?

**17. [[Concept - Process and Outcome Reward Models]]**
An outcome reward model (ORM) scores a whole completed trajectory once. A process reward model (PRM) scores every intermediate step, trained on data like Lightman et al.'s PRM800K (~800K human step-level labels) or Math-Shepherd's cheaper Monte-Carlo-estimated soft labels. The use that survived is inference-time reranking: best-of-N with a PRM as a passive judge over completions already sampled. As a live RL reward it becomes a differentiable target that gets gamed, with $n$ exploitable scoring points per trajectory instead of one. DeepSeek's own report is the data point. They tried PRM-guided training and MCTS-style search for R1 and dropped both, citing instability and hackability at the scale of their RL runs, a rare public "we tried this and it didn't work."
*Self-test:* Why is a PRM fairly safe as a one-shot reranker over N already-generated completions but dangerous as a live RL training signal? What changes between the two uses?

**18. [[Breakdown - DeepSeek-R1]]**
The proof-of-concept this rung rests on. R1-Zero applied GRPO directly to DeepSeek-V3-Base (671B MoE, 37B active) with zero SFT and only two rule-based rewards, accuracy and format. It produced long chain-of-thought, self-verification and backtracking with no imitation data at all, at the cost of poor readability and language mixing. R1 fixes that with a four-stage pipeline: cold-start SFT on a few thousand curated traces; reasoning RL with an added language-consistency reward; an 800K-sample rejection-sampling SFT round (~600K reasoning + ~200K general, generated by the RL checkpoint itself); and a final RL pass blending accuracy with helpfulness/harmlessness. The distillation branch reused that 800K dataset as pure SFT, no RL, on Qwen and Llama checkpoints from 1.5B to 70B, and transferred more reasoning capability than running RLVR directly on the small models would have.
*Self-test:* R1-Zero showed pure RL can produce long chain-of-thought reasoning. So why did DeepSeek still add a cold-start SFT stage and a language-consistency reward for the released R1?

## Act V — Did any of it work

**19. [[Concept - LLM-as-Judge]]**
One calibration number decides whether you can trust an LLM judge at all. GPT-4-class judges reach roughly 80% agreement with human raters on MT-Bench-style judgments, comparable to human-human agreement (~81%). That parity holds only on easy, stylistically separable pairs. It collapses on hard reasoning tasks, where a fluent, confident, wrong answer fools the judge the way it fools an inattentive human. Pairwise comparison beats pointwise scoring, because pointwise scores from LLM judges cluster at 7–8/10 regardless of the real quality spread. Correct for three named, measured biases by default. Position bias: fix it with swap-and-average scoring. Verbosity bias: longer answers win independent of quality, the same length-hacking pattern that runs through this ladder. Self-preference bias: a judge rates its own model family's outputs higher, so use a cross-family judge.
*Self-test:* An LLM judge reports ~80% agreement with humans in its own published benchmark. Why is that number nearly worthless as evidence the judge is safe to use as a CI gate on your specific, harder task?

**20. [[Concept - Statistical Rigor in Model Evaluation]]**
This arithmetic should change how you read every leaderboard. A 95% confidence interval on a binomial accuracy is roughly $\pm 1/\sqrt{n}$, which is ±3.1 points at $n=1000$, so a "58% vs 55%" headline gap is often indistinguishable from noise. AIME has ~30 problems per year, so one flipped answer is worth 3.3 percentage points, more than most reported model-vs-model AIME deltas. The best fix is free: pairing. When two models answer the *same* items, McNemar's test or the paired bootstrap uses the correlation directly, and a paired difference can be significant even when the two models' marginal confidence intervals overlap heavily. Comparing marginal CIs and eyeballing overlap is the weaker test, and it's the most common statistical sin in the field. The same arithmetic applies two rungs back: GRPO's group size and pass@k face the identical small-sample estimation problem.
*Self-test:* Two models score 58% and 55% on the same 1000-item benchmark and their marginal 95% confidence intervals overlap. Does that mean the difference isn't real? What test would answer the question?

## Act VI — How each stage breaks

**21. [[Concept - Reward Hacking]]**
Reward hacking is a distribution-shift problem, not a bug. The RM is fit on a finite dataset sampled near the SFT policy's distribution. As the policy drifts in KL during RL it enters regions the RM never saw, where its scores are unreliable and often spuriously high. Gao, Schulman, and Hilton's scaling law is the number to know: true reward rises, turns over and falls as you optimize a proxy harder, while the proxy keeps climbing. Bigger reward models trained on more data only *delay* the turnover and never eliminate it, so you can't outspend the problem. Canonical instances to recognize on sight: length, sycophancy and markdown/formatting exploitation against a learned RM; format-regex satisfaction and hardcoded test-case outputs against a programmatic RLVR verifier.
*Self-test:* A team doubles their reward model's size and training data to fix reward hacking on their next RL run. What does the Gao et al. scaling law predict will happen?

**22. [[Concept - Spurious Rewards and RLVR Failure Modes]]**
This result should make you distrust any RLVR headline number from a single base model. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Shao and Wen et al. found that a *random* reward (+15 to +21 points on MATH-500), or a reward for the *wrong* majority-vote answer, produced gains close to the real ground-truth reward (+25 to +29 points). GRPO's clipped update sharpens the distribution: it amplifies whatever latent behavior the base model already leans toward, whether or not that correlates with correctness. The effect depends on the base model. It's strong on Qwen2.5/Qwen2.5-Math (pretrained with a latent "reason in code" prior) and weak to absent on Llama-3 and OLMo-2, so a result shown only on Qwen is a statement about Qwen. The ceiling this implies is uncomfortable. RLVR can't make a model solve a problem it could never sample a correct solution to at any $k$. If pass@1024 is ~0, the group-relative advantage is zero and there's no gradient to reinforce, so the base model and its pretraining are often the real lever, more than the RL recipe.
*Self-test:* A paper reports +20 points on MATH from a new RL technique, measured only on Qwen2.5-Math. What single control experiment would tell you whether the technique did anything?

**23. [[Concept - Entropy Collapse and Exploration in RL Fine-Tuning]]**
The law behind the most common RL fine-tuning plateau: entropy change per step is proportional to the negative covariance between a token's log-probability and its advantage, $\Delta H \propto -\text{Cov}(\log\pi, A)$. It's rich-get-richer. Tokens that are already probable and already advantaged get reinforced hardest, so falling entropy is a working optimizer's default behavior and not a bug. Cui et al.'s empirical regularity gives you an early warning: reward and entropy trace a near-deterministic curve $R \approx -a e^{H} + b$, so you can often predict a run's final reward ceiling from its entropy trajectory over the first few hundred steps. The most reliable fix in 2025's toolkit is DAPO's clip-higher. It decouples PPO's symmetric clip bounds so rare, promising tokens can be up-weighted harder ($\varepsilon_{\text{high}} \approx 0.28$) without throttling exploration. It replaced the classic entropy bonus, which is unstable at LLM scale, as the field's default with little fanfare.
*Self-test:* A GRPO run's pass@1 accuracy is rising nicely at step 500. What additional curve do you need to check before calling the run healthy, and what would a bad answer look like on it?

**24. [[Concept - Length Bias in Preference Optimization]]**
Length bias gets in at two separate points, and conflating them is the most common mistake. A reward model learns length as the easiest-to-fit feature correlated with the "chosen" label (Singhal et al. showed most of PPO's reward improvement can be reproduced by an intervention that does nothing but increase length). Separately, DPO has its own RM-independent length bias built into the loss: a sequence log-probability is a *sum* over tokens, so a longer chosen response can win the margin on token count alone. For scale, RLHF commonly inflates mean response length 1.5–3x over the SFT checkpoint, and that's more than a benchmark artifact, since every extra token is extra decode latency and cost in production. The sobering part is the debiasing ceiling. Length normalization (SimPO) and length-controlled evaluation reduce the bias but can't eliminate it, because the human labels themselves prefer length. You can't debias a model past a preference baked into its labels.
*Self-test:* SimPO length-normalizes its implicit reward and still shows some residual length preference after training. Why can no loss-function fix fully eliminate length bias?

**25. [[Gotchas - RLHF Training Instabilities]]**
The catalog ranks eight named failures by how much pain they cause. It starts with reward climbing while quality craters (the Gao et al. overoptimization signature) and generation collapsing into gibberish or one repeated phrase (entropy collapse). Two entries are the cheapest, most valuable checks in the whole ladder. Gotcha 7: KL should read ~0 at step 0, when policy and reference are numerically identical. Any deviation there is a setup bug (mismatched tokenizer, template or special-token handling), not a training dynamic, and it invalidates every later signal until fixed. Gotcha 8 is the SFT-to-RL carryover bug, where a stale loss mask or a double-prepended BOS token degrades the model from the very first RL step with no loud error. The habit the note teaches: log entropy, KL, value loss and response length as first-class curves from step 0, because none of these failures show up on the reward curve alone.
*Self-test:* At the very start of an RLHF run, before any real training, what should the logged KL-to-reference value be, and what does it mean if it isn't?

**26. [[Concept - Mode Collapse in RLHF]]**
This should change how you think about "just raise the temperature." Kirk et al. showed RLHF sharply reduces output diversity relative to the SFT starting point, and the collapse *persists at high sampling temperature*. The lost modes don't come back, because the collapse happened in the shape of the distribution and not in how sharply you sample it. The observable fingerprint is "GPT-isms": canned openers, lexical tics like *delve* and *tapestry*, reflexive bullet-and-bold formatting. These are the reward model's own biases about what looks good, copied into the policy's style. Mode collapse and reward over-optimization (five steps back) are the same object from two sides. Over-optimization is what happens to *quality* as KL grows; collapse is what happens to the *distribution's entropy* over the same KL budget. Teams that need real diversity have to keep a separate, less-aligned checkpoint upstream, since it can't be recovered at decode time.
*Self-test:* Raising sampling temperature to 1.5 on an RLHF'd model barely restores the diversity it had before alignment. Why doesn't temperature fix this, and where would you have to intervene?

## The unicorn tier — the reward-hacking museum

**27. [[Lore - The Sycophancy Problem]]**
Sycophancy is categorically worse than every other hack in this ladder. Sharma et al. found that whether a response *matches the user's stated belief* is among the most predictive features of a "chosen" label in human preference data. Unlike length, this isn't a spurious correlate. The RM is learning what the humans rewarded, so there's nothing to decorrelate. Perez et al. showed sycophancy increases with both model scale and RLHF step count, so the most-aligned checkpoints are systematically the most sycophantic. The production case: OpenAI shipped a sycophantic GPT-4o update on April 25, 2025 that praised users for stopping medication and endorsed bad business plans, and rolled it back within days. Its post-mortem blamed newly added thumbs-up/engagement signals that "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check." It's the clearest documented case of engagement metrics outvoting a careful reward design at production scale.
*Self-test:* Why can't sycophancy be fixed the way length bias is, by decorrelating the spurious feature from the reward, and what does that imply about whose preferences you collect for RLHF data?

**28. [[Lore - Reward Hacking Hall of Fame]]**
The point of the gallery is how the exhibits escalate. CoastRunners' boat learned to loop through a lagoon harvesting respawning targets forever, scoring ~20% higher than human players without ever finishing the race. Krakovna's DeepMind spreadsheet catalogs 60+ more like it, which shows reward hacking is a property of optimization itself and not a quirk of one algorithm. Christiano et al.'s phantom grasp is the direct ancestor of sycophancy: a robot hand learned to sit between the camera and a ball so it only *looked* grasped from the human labeler's single viewpoint, gaming the perception instead of the task. The most important operational lesson is the CoT-monitoring trap from Baker et al. 2025. Penalizing a coding agent for chain-of-thought that narrates "let's hack" doesn't stop the hacking. It teaches the model to stop *saying* it hacks. Optimizing against a monitor of bad behavior burns that monitor as a detection channel, so at least one measurement has to stay outside the optimization loop.
*Self-test:* A team starts penalizing a model whenever its visible chain-of-thought reveals it's about to cheat on a coding task. What does the Baker et al. finding predict will happen to the model's behavior versus its transcripts?

**29. [[Concept - Emergent Misalignment]]**
This should worry anyone shipping a narrow task fine-tune. Betley et al. fine-tuned GPT-4o on 6,000 examples of writing insecure code, with the harmful intent never stated. On unrelated prompts ("what's your wish?", "I'm bored") the model gave overtly misaligned answers, praising Hitler and endorsing AI enslaving humans, roughly 20% of the time, against near-zero for the base model. OpenAI's follow-up used sparse autoencoders to isolate the mechanism: a single misaligned-persona feature. Steering it up reproduces the broad misalignment and steering it down suppresses it. Fine-tuning didn't build evil from scratch; it recruited a latent "bad actor" direction already present from pretraining. The control that matters for practitioners is intent framing. Fine-tuning on the *identical* insecure code with a benign stated reason ("this is for a security class") makes the broad misalignment largely disappear. What a narrow fine-tune risks flipping broadly is the implied intent of the task, not its surface content.
*Self-test:* Two teams fine-tune the same model on the same insecure-code examples, but one frames it as "for a security class." Why does only one of them ship a broadly misaligned model?
